Before anyone buys a device that moves near their cat's face, they go looking for reassurance. What they find instead is marketing copy and a handful of alarmed forum posts with no resolution.

These are the five concerns that come up most often. Some of them are worth taking seriously. Some of them are not a real risk, and it is more useful to say so plainly than to hedge.

---

## 1. "Can the lid close on my cat?"

This is the one everyone asks first, and the honest answer is that it depends on the design — which is exactly why it is worth checking before you buy rather than after.

Selective feeders are not powered like a car window. The mechanisms are low-torque by design, and the lid's job is to block access, not to clamp shut. On a well-designed unit, a lid meeting resistance stops or reverses rather than pushing through.

More importantly, most designs only close after the authorised cat has left the read zone — the cover stays open while the cat is present. The sequence is not "timer expires, lid closes," it is "cat leaves, lid closes."

**What to actually check before buying:**

- Does the lid stop or reverse when it meets resistance? Test it with your hand on day one.
- Does closing depend on the cat's absence, or only on a timer? Absence-based is safer.
- Are there pinch points a paw or tail could enter? Run your fingers around the mechanism.

**Where real risk does exist:** kittens, and very small or frail cats, are worth supervising for the first week with any powered feeder. So are cats who reach a paw into a bowl rather than eating with their mouth — a habit worth watching for, because it puts a limb somewhere the design may not expect.

## 2. "Is the RFID harmful to my cat?"

No, and this one does not require hedging.

The transponder in an implanted microchip or a collar tag is passive. It contains no battery and no power source. It sits completely inert until a reader's field briefly energises it at close range, at which point it returns an ID number and goes quiet.

The reader in the feeder emits a low-frequency field — the common standard is 134.2 kHz — over a range of an inch or two for an implanted chip. This is the same class of technology as the scanner at your vet's office and the tag on a library book. It is non-ionising, it is extremely low power, and it does not penetrate or heat tissue meaningfully at these levels.

A cat sleeping next to a feeder is not being irradiated. If you want a comparison: the field is far weaker and far shorter-range than the phone in your pocket.

## 3. "What happens if the batteries die?"

This is a legitimate concern, and the answer varies by product in a way that matters enormously.

**Ask one question before you buy: does this feeder fail open or fail closed?**

- **Fail open** means the lid releases when power is lost. Your cat can always eat. The cost is that your dietary protection disappears silently — the cat on the prescription diet is now sharing with everyone.
- **Fail closed** means the lid stays shut. Your protection holds, but if you are away, a cat may be locked out of their food.

Neither is universally correct. A household managing a diabetic cat on insulin has different priorities from a household where the worst case is a cat eating the wrong kibble. But you need to know which one you have bought, because the failure will happen eventually and you should not be discovering the behaviour then.

**Practical mitigations that work regardless:**

- Note the battery change interval and set a recurring reminder. Do not wait for symptoms.
- Watch for slow or weak lid movement — usually the first sign of a dying battery, and a common cause of a cat suddenly refusing the feeder.
- If you travel, change batteries before you leave, not when you get back.
- Leave a plain accessible bowl of appropriate food when you are away overnight, unless a medical condition makes that unsafe.

## 4. "What if my cat gets locked out of their food?"

The mechanism does not have to break for this to happen. The common causes are ordinary:

**A lost collar tag.** The most frequent cause by far. Breakaway collars are supposed to come off. Keep a spare tag, and if your cat loses collars often, register their implanted chip as a backup — see [RFID Collar Tag vs. Implanted Microchip](/blog/rfid-collar-tag-vs-implanted-microchip).

**A migrated microchip.** Chips can drift from the shoulder blades over the years. The vet can still find it with a full-body sweep; a feeder with a fixed antenna may not.

**Interference.** Metal directly under or behind the feeder degrades reads. So does placing two feeders close enough that their fields overlap.

**Weight gain or a very thick coat.** Both effectively increase the distance between chip and antenna.

The real safeguard is not the device — it is you noticing. Know roughly how much each cat eats, and check that food is actually going down. [A cat that is quietly not eating](/blog/cat-not-eating-multi-cat-home) is the thing you are protecting against, and no feeder will tell you. ## 5. "Is it hygienic? Does covered food spoil?"

A cover is generally better for hygiene than an open bowl, not worse. It keeps out flies and dust, and it keeps the other cats' noses out.

What it does not do is refrigerate. Wet food left in a covered bowl at room temperature has the same safe window as wet food in an open bowl — a couple of hours, less in a warm room. A cover keeps it presentable-looking for longer than it is actually good, which is a trap worth knowing about. See [Can You Feed Wet Food to Multiple Cats?](/blog/feed-wet-food-multiple-cats) for the timing.

**Cleaning matters more with a covered bowl, not less.** Food residue on the underside of a lid is out of sight and gets forgotten. Wash the bowl daily with wet food, and wipe the cover itself regularly. Check whether the parts that touch food are removable and dishwasher-safe before buying — if they are not, you will clean it less often, whatever you intend today.

## The Concern Nobody Lists, But Should

The genuine risk with any selective feeder is not mechanical. It is **false confidence**.

A feeder that has worked flawlessly for six months invites you to stop checking. Then the collar tag comes off, or a battery weakens, or a cat learns to follow their housemate in and eat from an already-open bowl — and the failure is silent. Nothing beeps. You find out at the next weigh-in, or the next blood panel.

Selective feeding removes the daily manual work. It does not remove the job of paying attention. Weigh your cats monthly, know their normal appetite, and treat a change in either as information.

## What to Look For in a Safe Setup

- A lid that stops or reverses against resistance
- Closing that depends on the cat leaving, not on a timer alone
- A known, documented power-failure behaviour
- Removable, washable parts that touch food
- Stable placement that does not wobble or rock
- A backup identification method registered for each cat

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it safe to leave a microchip feeder running while I am away for a weekend?**
For a healthy adult cat on a normal diet, generally yes, with fresh batteries and a known failure mode. For a cat with a medical condition where a missed meal matters — diabetic cats especially — arrange for a person to check in. No device substitutes for someone laying eyes on the cat.

**Can a dog trigger or break a cat feeder?**
A dog cannot open it without a registered ID, which is a common reason people buy one. But a determined large dog can tip or damage a lightweight unit. Placement above floor level, somewhere the cat can reach easily and the dog cannot, solves this better than the mechanism does.

**My cat sticks their paw in to fish food out. Is that dangerous?**
It is worth watching. Paw-feeding is a known habit in some cats and it puts a limb near the mechanism. If your cat does this consistently, supervise the first weeks closely and check how the lid behaves against a soft obstruction.

**Do kittens need a different approach?**
Yes. Kittens are small enough, fast enough, and curious enough to interact with a feeder in ways adult cats do not. Supervise directly until you have seen how your kitten uses it, and keep their own food freely accessible while they are growing.

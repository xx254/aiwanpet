It is 6:40 in the morning and your flat is about the size of a decent parking space. One cat is on the kitchen counter. One is wedged against your ankles. The third is sitting on the only clear bit of floor, between the fridge and the bin, watching the other two with a very flat expression. You have one bowl down and one bowl in your hand, and nowhere to put the second that is not within three feet of the first.

If you have ever read advice to "feed each cat in a separate room" and looked around at your studio or one-bedroom with a quiet sort of despair, this is for you. Learning how to feed multiple cats in a small apartment is mostly about making the space you have behave like more space. It can be done without a spare room, though it does take a bit of thought.

---

## Why Small Spaces Make Mealtimes Harder

In a big house, a nervous cat can eat around a corner and nobody notices. In a small flat, every bowl is in view of every other cat, and a stare across the room is as good as a shove.

The AAFP and ISFM [feline feeding consensus statement](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11343346) says cats "prefer to eat often and alone," and that in multi-cat homes "competition for food can cause conflict and pre-feeding aggression." It also notes that cats may not show "overt signs of tension or stress," so owners often miss it. In other words, a quiet meal is not proof that everyone is comfortable.

Think of it like a tiny café with one long shared table. Nobody is technically stopping anyone from eating, but the cat who feels watched will gulp, skip, or leave, and the confident one will drift over and finish what is left.

The same statement recommends that "feeding plans should include multiple feeding stations that are visually separated" and that stations "should not be close to litter boxes." In a small flat, the second point matters as much as the first, because the litter tray is often parked right next to the kitchen.

## Work Out Your Cats' Dynamics First

The consensus statement says the first task is to "determine the household group dynamics." You do not need a behaviour degree for that. Spend a few days noticing:

- **Who eats first, and who waits?** The waiter is often the cat who gets short-changed.
- **Who guards?** Guarding looks like standing over a bowl, blocking a doorway, or staring.
- **Who avoids whom?** The statement warns that forcing a cat to eat close to another cat it chooses to avoid can cause anxiety and stress.
- **Who can climb?** Placement should consider each cat's agility, since a shelf only works for a cat that can reach it comfortably.

Write it down if you like. A tired owner's memory at 6:40 a.m. is not a reliable witness.

## Ways to Create Separate Feeding Stations in a Small Space

You are aiming for what the statement calls "visually separated" stations. That does not require walls everywhere. It requires that a cat eating cannot easily see the others.

### Use the rooms you already have

Even a studio has a few usable spots. A bathroom, a hallway, a bedroom with a door, and a corner behind the sofa are all options. A bathroom is a good short-term feeding room if it is only used at mealtimes, with the door closed for the 15 to 20 minutes the cat takes to eat. Keep the bowl well away from the litter tray if the tray lives in there.

If you rent a one-bedroom, the bedroom and the kitchen already give you two stations on opposite sides of a closed door.

### Use height

Cats like vantage points. A sturdy shelf, the top of a cat tree or a wide windowsill can hold a bowl for a cat that jumps well. This gives you a station that is out of the floor traffic. It only suits agile cats. It is a poor idea for a senior cat with stiff joints, and a determined younger cat may follow it up there.

### Use barriers

A baby gate, a freestanding screen, a bookcase turned sideways or even a large cardboard box with a doorway cut in it can break the line of sight. The goal is not to wall a cat in, but to give it a place where its back is covered while it eats.

### Stagger meals

If you truly cannot separate by space, separate by time. Feed one cat in the kitchen while another is shut in the bedroom, then swap. It takes longer, and you need to be home for it. ## A Hypothetical Walk-Through: Three Cats, One Bedroom

Picture a one-bedroom flat with three cats. Biscuit is quick and bold and eats anything. Moth is shy and eats slowly. Pepper is a senior who needs a different food and does not jump any more.

A layout that could work:

1. **Biscuit** eats on a wide shelf by the living room window, where the height suits a confident jumper.
2. **Moth** eats in the bedroom with the door pulled shut for the length of the meal, so nobody is staring at the bowl.
3. **Pepper** eats on the floor in the bathroom, with the door closed and the litter tray moved to a different spot, so no one is rushing a senior cat.

Everyone is out of sight of everyone else at mealtime. There is no spare room, just three corners that each get used for 20 minutes. This is a made-up example, and your layout will look different, but the logic carries over: one station per cat, out of sight, away from the litter tray, fitted to what each cat can physically reach.

## Keep Water and Litter Out of the Picture

The consensus statement is explicit about litter boxes: feeding stations should not be close to them. In a small flat that can mean moving the tray, even a short way, so food and litter sit in different zones. If the only place for the tray is next to the kitchen, a screen between the two helps.

Set water out in a few places away from the food, rather than only next to the bowls. The statement mentions "multiple food and water stations" as part of the approach, though it does not give a distance, so do not take any number from me as a rule. What matters is that no cat has to walk past a rival to reach a drink.

## What About Cats That Steal?

Separate stations help, but the real test is the next ten minutes. A bold cat finishes quickly, then goes looking for second helpings. Some practical habits:

- **Pick up bowls after 15 to 20 minutes** if you are feeding scheduled meals, rather than leaving food down.
- **Stay in the room** for the first week so you can see who goes where.
- **Block the route**, not just the bowl. A cat that cannot reach the shy cat's room cannot reach the shy cat's bowl.

## Small Meals Suit Small Spaces

The same consensus statement says the daily allowance "should be split into multiple small meals" and fed throughout the day, using puzzle feeders when possible. In a small flat this has a side benefit. Shorter meals mean less time with food out, fewer chances for a stand-off, and less temptation for a quick raid.

It also fits a routine. Many owners find it easier to run three or four short feeding rounds than to supervise one long, tense meal. Just be sure each round has the same rule: each cat goes to its own station, and bowls leave the floor when the meal is over.

## When This Won't Work

Be honest about the limits. A studio with an open floor plan and no doors gives you very little to work with, and screens and shelves only go so far. Some cats will still find a way to see, smell or reach a rival's bowl. Staggered meals depend on you being home and awake, which is not realistic every day. And a cat with a medical need, like a prescription diet, can be put at risk by even one successful raid. Check with your vet about what is safe for that cat rather than relying on a layout. If one cat is clearly distressed at mealtimes, or one is losing weight while another gains it, that is a reason to talk to your vet, not to rearrange the furniture again.

## FAQ

### Can I feed multiple cats in the same room?

You can, but the AAFP/ISFM consensus statement recommends feeding stations that are visually separated, and notes that cats prefer to eat alone. In the same room, use a screen, a bookcase or different heights to break the line of sight, and place bowls as far apart as the room allows.

### How far apart should cat bowls be?

The consensus statement calls for "adequate distance" between stations but does not give a number. A practical test is whether a cat eating can see the other cats from its spot. If it can, add a barrier or move the bowl.

### Where should I put the cat food in a tiny apartment?

Away from the litter tray, according to the consensus statement, and somewhere each cat feels safe. Bathrooms, bedrooms, hallway corners and shelves are all common choices. Choose spots your cats can reach easily, particularly if one is old or has trouble jumping.

### What if I can't separate my cats for meals?

Try staggering the meals: feed one cat while the others are behind a closed door, then swap. Pick up bowls after the meal so nobody can graze on someone else's leftovers, and speak to your vet if one cat has a special diet.

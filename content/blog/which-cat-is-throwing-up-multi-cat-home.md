You come downstairs and there it is again: a small pile of half-digested food on the hallway rug, still slightly warm. Three cats are sitting around looking innocent. One is washing a paw. One is staring at you like breakfast is late. One has already left the room. You scoop it up, scrub the rug, and realise you have no idea which cat did it.

That is the problem with a cat throwing up in a multi-cat home. In a one-cat house, a vomit pile is a data point. In a shared house, it is a mystery, and a vet can't do much with "one of the three has been sick a few times this month."

This guide covers how to work out which cat is throwing up, how to read what you find, and when it's time to stop investigating and call the vet.

---

## First, Is It Vomiting or Regurgitation?

Before you play detective, check what actually came up. Vets separate two things that look similar on the floor.

VCA's guide to [vomiting in cats](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/vomiting-in-cats) says that "vomiting typically involves abdominal contractions and effort," while regurgitation "typically occurs quickly, without abdominal contractions" and "often occurs immediately after eating or drinking." VCA adds that regurgitation is usually associated with problems affecting the esophagus.

In practice:

- **Regurgitation:** food comes back up almost straight away, with little effort.
- **Vomiting:** the cat visibly strains, with the belly contracting, before anything comes up.

If you can catch the moment on your phone, do. VCA notes that a video of the behaviour helps your vet tell vomiting from coughing, which is a different problem altogether.

## Why the Culprit Is Hard to Spot in a Shared Home

Cats don't announce it. Many wait until you're out of the room, and they often pick the same few spots: behind the sofa, the corner of a rug, the bottom of the stairs.

Eating habits make it harder to read. The AAFP and ISFM [feline feeding consensus statement](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11343346) notes that "in multi-cat households, cats usually do not share their provided space equally," and that modern pet food "is highly palatable and easy to eat rapidly, due to its small chunks and kibble formulation." Put those together and you get a familiar scene: one cat bolts its bowl, then drifts over to a neighbour's, and ends up with a big meal in a hurry.

It's like someone gulping a whole sandwich on a moving bus. Sometimes it stays down and sometimes it doesn't, and by the time you notice, you can't tell who was sitting where.

## How to Find Out Which Cat Is Throwing Up

You don't need special gear. You need a few days and a habit of writing things down.

### 1. Start a simple log

Keep a note on your phone. For every incident, write the date, the time you found it, the spot, what it looked like, and what each cat had eaten that day. After a week or two, patterns tend to show up on their own.

### 2. Look at the contents

The pile itself is a clue:

- **Hair mixed in:** probably a hairball. VCA says it's normal for cats to swallow hair while grooming and to "vomit hairballs periodically," but also that if a cat is "vomiting hairballs more than once a month," you should contact your vet.
- **Undigested food, soon after a meal:** fits regurgitation, and often a cat that ate too fast.
- **Foam, liquid, or anything unusual:** note it down and mention it to your vet rather than guessing at the cause.
- **Something that isn't food** (string, plant matter, plastic): treat this as urgent. VCA lists suspected ingestion of a foreign object or toxic substance among the reasons to seek veterinary care immediately.

### 3. Match the food to the cat

If the cats eat different foods, the vomit may give the culprit away. A pile of grey pâté in a home where only one cat gets pâté is an easy answer. If everyone eats the same kibble, you'll have to work harder.

For a home with mixed diets, this is another reason to feed cats apart. Our guide on [how to feed multiple cats separately](/blog/how-to-feed-multiple-cats-separately) walks through the practical options.

### 4. Feed separately and stay nearby afterwards

This is the most reliable method. Feed each cat in its own space, out of sight of the others, then stay close for a while after the meal. The AAFP/ISFM statement recommends that "feeding plans should include multiple feeding stations that are visually separated," and says that "just because cats will eat together does not mean that they would not greatly benefit from separate feeding areas."

If you can, keep each cat in its own room for a short time after eating. Any vomit that turns up in a closed room has an owner.

### 5. Make evidence easy to find

If you can't confine the cats, put washable mats near the usual hiding places. Fresh vomit is easier to read than something that has dried for hours, and you'll find it sooner.

### 6. Look at the cats, not only the mess

Check each cat's coat, weight and energy over the same period. Weigh everyone now and write down the numbers. A cat who is losing weight, hiding, or leaving food is worth a closer look, and your vet will want those notes.

## What the Pattern Might Mean

A log with a few weeks of entries usually points one of three ways.

### Always soon after eating, with undigested food

This often suggests the cat is eating too fast, or too much in one go. Slowing meals down is the usual first step. Our post on [how to stop a cat from eating too fast](/blog/stop-cat-eating-too-fast) covers it in detail. The AAFP/ISFM statement also says that "the cat's daily food allowance should be split into multiple small meals and fed throughout the 24 h period," and that cats "naturally prefer to eat small, frequent meals alone."

If you want ideas for splitting up the day, see [Best Feeding Schedule for Multiple Cats](/blog/best-feeding-schedule-multiple-cats).

### Hairballs on a regular basis

Occasional hairballs are normal. Frequent ones, past the once a month VCA mentions, are worth a vet visit rather than a new brush.

### Random, repeated, or with other changes

Vomiting that comes and goes with no clear link to meals, or that comes with weight loss, hiding or a change in appetite, needs a vet. Don't try to solve this one at home. If you've also noticed that one cat seems to be eating less, read [My Cat Isn't Eating: How to Tell Which Cat Skipped a Meal](/blog/cat-not-eating-multi-cat-home).

## When to Call the Vet

Treat these as call-today signs:

- **Blood in the vomit.** VCA lists this among the signs for seeking care immediately.
- **A cat that may have swallowed a foreign object or something toxic.**
- **Vomiting that continues.** VCA says that if a cat "continues to vomit for more than one to two days," you should seek veterinary care.

Beyond those, call your vet if the log shows a pattern you can't explain, even if each episode looks small.

Take your notes with you: dates, times, what came up, which foods were eaten, and the weights. Add a short video of the episode if you managed to record one. This turns "one of my cats throws up sometimes" into something a vet can work with.

## Making Sure You Know Who Is Eating What

Everything above gets easier if each cat reliably eats only its own food, at its own pace. A few options, with the honest trade-offs:

- **Closed doors at mealtimes.** Free and effective, but someone needs to be home and watching the clock for every meal.
- **Slow-feed bowls or puzzle bowls.** Useful for a fast eater, but a determined cat can still raid a neighbour's bowl.
- **Separate heights.** A high shelf for one cat only works if the others can't or won't jump up.
- **A selective feeder** that opens only for the cat it's set up for. Our explainer on [whether microchip feeders actually work](/blog/do-microchip-cat-feeders-work) covers the trade-offs.

## When This Won't Work

A log and a few separate meals can narrow things down, but they can't diagnose anything. Some cats vomit away from meals, at night, or when you're out, and you may never catch them in the act. Shutting cats in separate rooms can also stress a nervous cat, or shift the problem somewhere else, which muddies your notes for a few days. And if the cause is medical, no feeding set-up will fix it. Treat your detective work as a way to give your vet better information, not as a replacement for the appointment.

## FAQ

### How can I tell which of my cats is throwing up?

Keep a log of when and where you find vomit and what each cat ate. Then feed the cats separately and keep each in its own room for a while after meals. Any vomit found in a closed room belongs to the cat inside. A video of the episode helps your vet too.

### Is it normal for a cat to throw up after eating?

Not as a regular thing. VCA says it's normal for cats to vomit hairballs periodically, but more than once a month is a reason to contact your vet. Food coming back up soon after meals is worth mentioning to your vet, even if it seems minor.

### What's the difference between a cat vomiting and regurgitating?

VCA explains that vomiting typically involves abdominal contractions and effort, while regurgitation typically occurs quickly, without abdominal contractions, and often right after eating or drinking. Regurgitation is usually associated with problems affecting the esophagus.

### When is cat vomit an emergency?

VCA advises seeking veterinary care immediately if there is blood in the vomit or if the cat may have swallowed a foreign object or toxic substance. It also says to seek care if a cat keeps vomiting for more than one to two days.

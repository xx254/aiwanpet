It is 6:40 in the morning. The cat carrier is by the front door, the appointment is at 8:00, and the vet's instructions say *no food after midnight*. You have done everything right. Then you walk into the kitchen and find the patient sitting in the other cat's bowl, chewing the last of the kibble you left out for everyone else.

If you are trying to work out how to fast a cat before surgery when you have other cats, this is the scene you are trying to avoid. One cat needs an empty stomach. The others need breakfast, and some of them may need their own schedule kept for medical reasons. They all share one kitchen, and the hungry one is usually the most determined cat in the house.

Here is how to plan the night before and the morning of so the fast actually holds, and what to tell your vet if it does not.

---

## Why Fasting Matters, and Why the Number Varies

Food is withheld before an anaesthetic mainly to reduce the chance of vomiting or regurgitation while the cat is sedated. VCA's general surgery guidance puts it plainly: ["To prevent vomiting, you should withhold food from your pet after midnight the night before surgery."](https://vcahospitals.com/shelden/services/primary-care/general-surgery)

The exact number of hours is less fixed than many owners assume. The [AAHA fasting and treatment recommendations](https://www.aaha.org/wp-content/uploads/globalassets/02-guidelines/2020-anesthesia/aahaanesthesiaguidelines_fastingandtreatmentrecommendations.pdf), which accompany the 2020 AAHA Anesthesia and Monitoring Guidelines for Dogs and Cats, give different windows for different patients:

- **Healthy adult cats:** food withheld for 4–6 hours, with free access to water.
- **Kittens under 8 weeks or under 2 kg:** food withheld for no longer than 1–2 hours.
- **Diabetic cats:** food withheld for 2–4 hours, with half a meal given 2–4 hours beforehand, alongside an adjusted insulin dose.
- **Cats with a history of, or at risk for, regurgitation:** a longer fast of 6–12 hours, with water also withheld for 6–12 hours.

AAHA notes that the table is a guideline, not a standard of care. Your own vet may ask for something different based on your cat, the procedure, and when in the day they are operating. **Follow your clinic's instructions over anything you read here, including this article.** What this post can help with is the part your clinic cannot do for you: making those instructions hold in a house full of other cats.

It is also worth knowing that fasting before **bloodwork** is a separate instruction. The same VCA page asks owners to fast pets 8–12 hours before bloodwork. If your cat is going in for a blood test rather than a procedure, check which instruction applies.

## The Real Problem: Fasting Is a One-Cat Rule in a Shared House

Most fasting advice assumes one pet. In a multi-cat home, the instruction "no food after midnight" quietly turns into one of three things:

1. **Everyone fasts.** Simple and reliable, and unfair on cats with no appointment. It may also be unsafe for a housemate who is diabetic, very young, or on medication that has to be given with food.
2. **Nobody's food is managed and you hope for the best.** This is how the scene at the top of the article happens.
3. **The patient is separated from all food.** The right goal, but it takes some planning.

Think of it like a house with one person on a pre-surgery fast and three roommates who still need to eat. You would not lock the whole fridge. You would make sure the one person who must not eat cannot reach anything. The same logic applies to cats, except that cats cannot be trusted to keep the promise.

## A Night-Before Plan That Holds

### Step 1: Confirm the exact instructions in writing

Before the day itself, ask the clinic three questions and write the answers down:

- What time should the last meal be?
- Is water allowed, and until when?
- Should any regular medication be given that morning, and if so, with how much food?

The AAHA table notes that oral medications can be given with a small amount of wet food, around 1–2 tablespoons, or coated in a paste. That is a decision for your vet, not something to assume.

### Step 2: End free feeding the night before

If your household leaves a bowl of kibble down all day, that bowl is the single biggest risk to the fast. Pick it up at the same time as the patient's last meal. The other cats can have a measured evening meal instead.

For many homes this is a small, useful change anyway. See [Free Feeding vs Scheduled Feeding for Multiple Cats](/blog/free-feeding-vs-scheduled-feeding-multiple-cats) for the trade-offs.

### Step 3: Choose where the patient sleeps

The most reliable option is to put the fasting cat in a single room overnight with water, a litter tray, a bed, and nothing edible. A bathroom or spare bedroom works. Check for anything a hungry cat might chew: treats in a drawer, a forgotten bag of kibble, a houseplant, or food left on a bedside table.

If the patient is the cat who struggles with being shut in, a quiet room with you in it may be calmer than a closed door with a cat crying behind it. The goal is no food, not maximum isolation.

The opposite approach also works in some homes: keep the patient in the main living space and feed the other cats in a closed room. Which way round is better depends on which cats tolerate a closed door. See [Do You Need a Separate Room to Feed Cats Differently?](/blog/separate-room-feed-cats-differently) for how to set that up.

### Step 4: Clear the backup food sources

Hungry cats are resourceful. Before bed, check:

- Countertops and the sink
- Dog bowls, if you have a dog
- Bins with loose lids
- The other cats' leftover wet food
- Treat jars and pouches within reach of a jumping cat

If your patient is a known food thief, assume they will find anything you miss. The patterns described in [Why Is My Cat Stealing the Other Cat's Food](/blog/cat-stealing-other-cats-food) do not take a night off.

## The Morning Of

### Feed the other cats where the patient cannot follow

Breakfast for everyone else should happen behind a closed door, or at least out of the patient's sight and reach. Pick up every bowl as soon as each cat finishes. A half-eaten pouch left on the floor for ten minutes is long enough.

If you share the house with another person, agree on who feeds which cat and where. Two well-meaning people each "just putting a bit down" is a common way for a fast to fail.

### Leave water down unless told otherwise

For healthy cats, AAHA's table allows free access to water, and VCA's page says you do not need to withhold it. The exception is a cat your vet considers at risk of regurgitation, where water may also be restricted. Follow the clinic's instruction exactly.

### Give morning medication only as instructed

If your cat takes daily medication, the clinic will usually tell you whether to give it and how. Thyroid tablets, pain relief, and insulin all have different rules on surgery day. For diabetic cats, AAHA's recommendation involves a reduced meal and a reduced insulin dose, which is exactly the kind of detail that needs to come from your vet. For the everyday side of giving medication in a shared home, see [How to Medicate a Cat in a Multi-Cat Home](/blog/medicating-cat-multi-cat-home).

## When Another Cat Is the Complication

Sometimes the hard case is not the patient but a housemate:

- **A diabetic housemate** needs to eat on schedule. Do not make them fast in solidarity. Feed them in a separate room at their usual time.
- **A kitten in the house** should not be made to skip meals either. AAHA's own guidance shows how short a safe fast is for very young cats.
- **A cat on a prescription diet** still needs that diet. See [How to Feed Prescription Cat Food in a Multi-Cat Home](/blog/prescription-cat-food-multiple-cats).

In all three cases the answer is the same: the patient is the cat who is restricted. Nobody else's routine should bend around the appointment.

---

## What to Do if the Fast Breaks

It happens. A cat opens a cupboard, or a housemate's bowl was not picked up in time.

**Tell the clinic before you leave the house, or as soon as you arrive.** Say roughly what was eaten, how much, and when. Do not leave it out because you are worried they will cancel. Your vet may decide to delay the procedure, move it later in the day, or go ahead with extra precautions. That decision belongs to them, and they can only make it with accurate information.

**Do not try to make the cat vomit.** Inducing vomiting at home is not a fix for a broken fast and can cause harm of its own.

---

## When This Won't Work

Some situations are hard to solve with planning alone, and it is better to say so.

In a studio flat or a very small home with no door that closes between the cats, physical separation may not be possible. Some cats cry, scratch, or panic when shut in, and some owners will reasonably decide a stressed cat is a worse outcome. Some households have so many cats on so many schedules that the morning becomes unmanageable.

In those cases, talk to the clinic. Ask whether your cat can be admitted the evening before, or whether the procedure can be scheduled as the first case of the day so the fast is shorter and easier to protect. AAHA's table already recommends a first-of-the-day slot for diabetic cats and very young or small patients.

## FAQ

### Can my other cats eat while my cat is fasting before surgery?

Yes. Only the cat having the procedure needs to fast. Feed the others somewhere the patient cannot reach, and pick up their bowls as soon as they finish. The other cats should not miss meals, especially if they are diabetic, very young, or on medication given with food.

### How long should a cat fast before surgery?

It depends on the cat and the clinic. AAHA's 2020 recommendations suggest 4–6 hours for a healthy adult cat, shorter for kittens and diabetic cats, and longer for cats at risk of regurgitation. VCA's general advice is to withhold food after midnight the night before. Always follow the exact instructions from your own vet.

### Can my cat drink water before surgery?

In most cases, yes. AAHA's table allows free access to water for healthy cats, and VCA says water does not need to be withheld. Your vet may restrict water for a cat with a history of regurgitation, so check if you are unsure.

### What if my cat ate the other cat's food the morning of surgery?

Call the clinic and tell them what was eaten and when. They may delay the anaesthetic or change the plan. Do not hide it, and do not try to make your cat vomit.

### Should I fast my cat before a blood test too?

Often, yes, but the instruction is different from surgery. VCA's guidance for bloodwork is an 8–12 hour fast. Ask your clinic which applies to your appointment.

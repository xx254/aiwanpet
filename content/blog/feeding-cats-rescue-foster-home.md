Advice written for a two-cat household falls apart at ten. "Feed them in separate rooms" assumes you have enough rooms. "Supervise every meal" assumes meals do not overlap. "Weigh them monthly" assumes you can tell them apart on a chart.

People running rescue and foster situations out of their own homes — often on a pension, often alone — are solving a different problem from the one most pet blogs describe. This is written for them.

We published [one such story](/blog/she-gave-her-later-years-to-the-cats-left-behind) recently: a woman who has taken cats in since 2009 and still cares for forty or fifty of them at seventy-five. The system below is the shape that situations like hers tend to settle into.

---

## Zoning: The Single Most Important Decision

Almost every functional large-cat household ends up organised by zone rather than by individual. You are not managing twelve feeding plans. You are managing three or four groups.

A structure that works:

**Zone 1 — Healthy adults.** The largest group, on the same maintenance diet, free-fed or scheduled together. No individual protection needed. These cats subsidise your attention budget: because they need almost none, you have some left for everyone else.

**Zone 2 — Seniors and chronic conditions.** Renal diets, thyroid medication, cats who eat slowly and get displaced. This group needs the most individual attention, and it is the group most worth keeping physically close to where you sleep, because they are the ones who need checking at night.

**Zone 3 — Medical and quarantine.** New arrivals, cats on antibiotics, post-surgical recovery, anything contagious. Separate room, separate bowls, separate cleaning order.

**Zone 4 — Kittens.** Growth diets, frequent meals, and no access to adult food or medication.

Zoning converts an impossible problem into three manageable ones. The cats in Zone 1 do not need selective feeders. The cats in Zone 2 largely do.

## Work Out Your Actual Constraint

With a dozen cats, the binding constraint is rarely food cost. It is one of these:

**Time.** Twelve cats, three zones, twice a day, plus medication rounds, plus cleaning. Anything that requires you to be physically present at a specific moment is expensive, and anything that runs unattended is worth disproportionately more than it costs.

**Physical capacity.** Bending, lifting, carrying, and getting up and down repeatedly. For an older caregiver this is the real limit, and it is the one that tightens with time. Setups that require carrying bowls to separate rooms twice a day are the first thing to fail when a caregiver's health changes.

**Space.** Room separation does not scale. Four rooms and twelve cats means the maths stops working somewhere around the third special diet.

Design around whichever of these is actually binding for you. Most advice optimises for money because that is easy to write about, and money is frequently not the constraint that breaks the system.

## Where Selective Feeding Earns Its Place

You do not need a protected bowl for every cat. You need one wherever a specific cat's intake has to be guaranteed or prevented — usually a small minority of a large group.

**The renal or prescription cat in a mixed group.** The therapeutic diet is expensive and only works if it is most of what that cat eats. Without protection, a dozen cats share the most expensive food in the house.

**The cat who must not eat something.** Food-trial cats and cats with genuine allergies need protection from everyone else's bowls, which is a harder layout than protecting their own.

**The frail or slow eater.** In a large group, the cat who eats slowly is the cat who does not eat. Protected access lets them return to their own food four hours later.

**Medication in food.** In a group setting, a medicated bowl is a genuine hazard — the dose reaching the wrong cat is far more likely than in a two-cat home. See [How to Medicate a Cat in a Multi-Cat Home](/blog/medicating-cat-multi-cat-home).

Because covers like the [Aiwan Cat Food Shield](https://aiwanpet.com) fit over bowls you already own, scaling is incremental: you add protection to the two or three bowls that need it rather than replacing feeding equipment for the whole house. In a rescue setting that also means a shield can be reassigned when a cat is adopted and a new arrival takes their place.

## Identification When the Population Changes

This is the practical detail that separates rescue feeding from household feeding: your cats change.

**RFID collar tags are usually the right default here**, not implanted microchips. Tags can be reassigned in minutes. Chips require every arrival to be scanned, the format checked, and the ID registered — and many arrivals come with no chip, an unreadable chip, or no records at all. See [RFID Collar Tag vs. Implanted Microchip](/blog/rfid-collar-tag-vs-implanted-microchip).

Keep spare tags. In a twelve-cat house, one is always missing.

**Keep a written register.** Name, description, diet, medication, weight history, which tag, which zone. In a large group, memory is not a system, and if you are ever ill, someone else has to be able to run your house from that sheet.

## Cost Control That Doesn't Compromise Care

**Protect the expensive food first.** The single highest-return change in a large household is putting a cover on the therapeutic diet. Prescription food shared among a group is the largest avoidable expense most rescuers have. The arithmetic is in [Is a Selective Cat Feeder Worth It?](/blog/is-a-selective-cat-feeder-worth-it).

**Buy the maintenance diet in bulk, not the therapeutic one.** Bulk works for the large stable group. Therapeutic diets have shorter practical shelf lives once open and are often needed in smaller amounts than a bulk order assumes.

**Ask your vet about generics and about which prescription diets are genuinely necessary.** Some are, some are habit. A vet who knows you are running a rescue on a private budget can often restructure a plan around that reality.

**Look into rescue pricing.** Some manufacturers, veterinary practices and food banks operate discount or donation programmes for registered rescues and active fosterers. It is worth asking directly — these are frequently not advertised.

## The Part That Gets Skipped

**Weigh them.** In a group of twelve, weight is the only early-warning system you have. Nobody notices a cat quietly losing 15% over two months in a crowd; a scale does, immediately. Once a month, written down, one line per cat.

**Watch one full meal a day.** Not all of them — one. Rotate which zone. You are looking for who is not eating, who is being displaced, and who is guarding. Ten minutes of actually watching beats an hour of assumptions.

**Keep the new arrivals separate properly.** Quarantine is a feeding decision as much as a health one: separate bowls, separate room, and cleaned last in the order so you are not carrying anything through the house.

## Be Honest About Capacity

This is the hardest section to write and the most important one.

Every experienced rescuer eventually faces the point where the number of cats exceeds what can be cared for properly, and the number almost always grows by one reasonable decision at a time. Nobody plans to be over capacity.

Practical markers worth checking yourself against: are all cats getting the individual observation they need? Can you cover veterinary care for a serious illness in one cat without it compromising the others? Could you run this household if you were ill for two weeks?

Systems and equipment raise your capacity. They do not make it unlimited, and the honest answer is sometimes that the next cat has to go to someone else. That is not a failure of compassion. It is what protects the cats already in your care.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How many feeding stations do I need for ten cats?**
More than you think, and spread out. The usual guidance for multi-cat homes is one station per cat plus one, and while that is rarely achievable at ten, the direction is right: more stations, further apart, with broken sight lines. Concentrating a large group at one feeding point produces conflict that has nothing to do with the food.

**Is free feeding acceptable with a large group?**
For a group of healthy adults on the same maintenance diet, it is often the most practical approach and it reduces mealtime tension. The problem is that it removes your daily visual check, so pair it with scheduled wet food once a day where you actually watch.

**How do I stop the whole group eating the prescription food?**
Protect that bowl specifically. In a large group, room separation for one cat at every meal is not sustainable, and the diet only works if it is most of what that cat eats.

**What is the best way to keep track of who needs what?**
A written chart on the wall where the food is stored, not an app and not memory. Name, diet, medication, tag number, weight. Anyone who steps in to help should be able to feed your house correctly from that sheet alone.

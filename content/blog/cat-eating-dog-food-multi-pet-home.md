The dog has just been fed, and he has gone to the water bowl for a drink. By the time he turns around, the tabby is standing with both front paws on the rim of his dish, chewing fast with her ears flat. You hear the clink of kibble against the steel. She has her own full bowl across the room. She does not want it.

If your cat keeps eating the dog's food, you are probably wondering two things: is it hurting her, and how do you make it stop? The short version is that a stolen mouthful is usually not a problem, but a regular habit is. This post covers why cats go for dog food, what the difference really is, and practical ways to keep each animal at its own bowl, including in a home that also has other cats.

## Is It Safe for a Cat to Eat Dog Food?

### A few stolen bites are not an emergency

PetMD's article [Can Cats Eat Dog Food?](https://www.petmd.com/cat/nutrition/can-cats-eat-dog-food), written by a veterinarian, says a cat can eat a small amount of dog food without toxicity or lasting effects. It also says a nibble of stolen dog food will not harm a cat, though it will not help her health either. If you caught her once, or she grabs a few pieces now and then, you do not need to panic.

### A regular habit is different

The same article is clear that cats cannot be maintained on a dog food diet. It explains that cats are strict carnivores with nutritional needs that dog foods are not built to meet. According to the article:

- **Taurine.** Cats cannot make their own and must get it from food. A lack of it can lead to a weakened heart muscle, loss of vision and digestive problems. PetMD says all commercially available cat food has taurine added, but it is rarely included in dog foods.
- **Protein.** PetMD gives typical dog food as roughly 18 to 26 percent protein as fed, and suggests cats do better with at least 30 to 34 percent, or more in canned food.
- **Vitamin A and other nutrients.** Cats cannot make vitamin A, arachidonic acid or niacin from other ingredients the way dogs can, so they have to get them in ready-made form. The article says dog foods will never contain enough vitamin A for optimal cat nutrition, and that they are seldom supplemented with arachidonic acid.

The article warns that if a cat is fed only dog food for a long period, the consequences can be serious. That is mostly a concern for a cat who has swapped her own food for the dog's. A cat who eats her own meals and takes a few extra pieces from the dog is in a much better position.

### Check the label on both foods

Neither pet's food should be assumed to suit the other. PetMD also points to life stage on the label, such as growth, adult maintenance or all life stages, as something to match to the animal. If you are not sure what your own cat food covers, your vet can tell you.

## Why Cats Go for the Dog's Bowl

Knowing the reason makes the fix easier.

### It smells and tastes interesting

Cats are curious, and anything another animal is eating is worth investigating. Some cats simply like the taste. Others like the crunch or the novelty.

### It is a quick, unguarded meal

If your cat is on a measured diet, she may be genuinely hungry. If you have been wondering [why your cat is always hungry](/blog/why-is-my-cat-always-hungry), a source of extra calories in the kitchen could be part of the answer.

### Her own food is hard to get to

In a home with several cats and a dog, a timid cat may find her own bowl occupied by a bolder cat. The dog's bowl, on the floor in a quiet corner, can be an easy alternative. If that sounds familiar, [my cat gets chased away from the food bowl](/blog/cat-chased-away-from-food-bowl-multi-cat-home) has ideas for helping a cat who keeps losing out.

### It is a habit

A cat who is rewarded once with a full bowl of unguarded kibble will try again. Repetition makes it a routine.

## How to Stop It

None of these needs special equipment. Start with the simplest one that fits your home.

### Feed the dog somewhere the cat cannot go

Feed the dog in a room with the door closed, or behind a baby gate the cat cannot jump over, then pick the bowl up when he is done. A bowl that is picked up leaves nothing to steal.

For most households this is the single most effective change. It does mean being present at mealtimes, which is less easy if you leave early or come home late.

### Raise the cat's food, not the dog's

The opposite problem is also common: the dog eating the cat's food. [How to keep your dog out of the cat food](/blog/keep-dog-out-of-cat-food) covers that direction in detail. A shelf, a cat tree or a counter your dog cannot reach keeps cat food away from him. A cat who is good at jumping can still reach the dog's bowl on the floor, so raising the cat's bowl does nothing for this particular problem.

### Make mealtimes predictable

Pick up leftovers at a set time and keep to it. Free-fed bowls give the cat constant opportunities. Scheduled meals give both animals a clear signal about when and where to eat. [Free feeding vs scheduled feeding for multiple cats](/blog/free-feeding-vs-scheduled-feeding-multiple-cats) explains the trade-offs, and most of it applies when one of the "cats" is a dog.

### Check the cat's portion

A cat who hunts for extra food may be getting less than she needs. Weigh a day's food and see how it compares with what the packet and your vet recommend. [How much should I feed my cat](/blog/how-much-to-feed-each-cat-multi-cat-home) works through how to find each cat's number. If the cat is on a diet because she is overweight, snacks from the dog's bowl can quietly cancel the effort.

### Feed the cat at the same time

If the cat gets her meal at the same time as the dog, she is less likely to be tempted. Put her bowl somewhere calm, ideally away from the dog and from other cats. [How to feed multiple cats separately](/blog/how-to-feed-multiple-cats-separately) lays out options for different homes.

### Do not punish her

Shouting or spraying water tends to teach a cat to eat faster when you are not looking. It also adds stress at mealtimes. Calm management works better than reprimands.

## A Hypothetical Scenario

Picture a flat with a beagle, a young cat called Biscuit and an older cat called Moss. The dog is fed twice a day on the kitchen floor. Biscuit has worked out that if she gets there as the bowl goes down, she gets several mouthfuls before anyone notices.

The owner starts feeding the dog in the hallway with the kitchen door shut, then collects the bowl after ten minutes. Biscuit gets her own meal in the kitchen at the same time, so she is occupied. Within a week the habit has mostly faded. Moss, who eats slowly, is still fed in the bedroom so Biscuit cannot move in on her bowl.

This is invented, but it shows how little needs to change: a door, a timer and a pick-up routine.

## When to Talk to Your Vet

Talk to your vet if:

- The cat has been eating mostly dog food for weeks, not just stealing a few bites.
- She is vomiting, has diarrhoea, seems lethargic or is losing weight.
- She is on a prescription or therapeutic diet. Even small amounts of non-prescription food can undermine it. See [how to feed prescription cat food in a multi-cat home](/blog/prescription-cat-food-multiple-cats) for how to keep that diet protected.
- Your cat has a health condition and you are not sure how much dog food matters.

PetMD's article does not cover what to do in these situations, so this is a point to raise with your own vet rather than rely on general advice.

## When This Won't Work

Closed doors and picked-up bowls do not suit every home.

- **Free-fed dogs.** If the dog grazes all day, there is no neat mealtime to manage. You would need to change how he is fed, which may not be possible for him.
- **Small spaces.** In a studio flat there may be no door to close. You may have to feed one animal in a crate or on a raised surface.
- **Determined cats.** Some cats will jump any barrier. A different location, or feeding the dog while the cat is occupied elsewhere, may be the only answer.
- **Unsupervised hours.** If nobody is home at mealtimes, you need either a timed arrangement or someone else to help.

## FAQ

### Will dog food hurt my cat if she eats it once or twice?

According to PetMD, a small amount of dog food will not cause toxicity or lasting effects in a cat. A habit is the thing to avoid, because dog food does not meet a cat's needs.

### Why is dog food bad for cats long term?

PetMD explains that cats need nutrients such as taurine, vitamin A, arachidonic acid and niacin in forms dog food often does not supply enough of, and that they need more protein than most dog foods contain. Long-term dog food feeding can lead to serious problems.

### What if my cat prefers the dog's food to her own?

Check that her own food is fresh, her bowl is clean and her portion is enough. If she still refuses it, speak to your vet, because a sudden change in appetite can have medical causes. You can also try switching her food gradually; [how to switch cat food when you have multiple cats](/blog/switch-cat-food-multiple-cats) covers how.

### Can I feed my cat and dog the same food to make life easier?

PetMD says cats cannot be maintained on a dog food diet. Feeding one food to both is not advisable unless it is formulated for both, and your vet would need to confirm that.

### What if my dog eats the cat's food instead?

That is a separate problem with its own fixes. See [how to keep your dog out of the cat food](/blog/keep-dog-out-of-cat-food).

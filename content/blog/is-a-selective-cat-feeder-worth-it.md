A selective feeder is not an impulse purchase. It costs more than a bowl, and the benefit — food going into the correct cat — is invisible when it works.

So here is the arithmetic, honestly done, including the cases where the answer is no.

---

## Cost 1: The Prescription Food Your Healthy Cat Is Eating

This is the line item people underestimate, because the bag empties and nobody asks who emptied it.

Therapeutic diets — renal, urinary, hydrolysed protein, satiety formulas — typically run somewhere in the range of **two to three times the price of a good non-prescription food**. Depending on the brand, the format, and whether your cat is on wet, dry, or both, many households are spending somewhere between **$50 and $150 a month** to feed one cat a prescribed diet. Your own number is on your last receipt, and that is the number to use.

Now the theft. In a two-cat house where the healthy cat has free access to the prescription bowl, the healthy cat is rarely taking a token nibble. Prescription food is designed to be palatable, because sick cats need to want to eat it. A confident, faster, healthier cat frequently takes **a quarter to a half** of it.

Run it on your own numbers:

> Prescription food: **$90/month**
> Healthy cat's share: **35%**
> Wasted: **$31.50/month → $378/year**

That alone pays for most selective feeding setups inside a year — and that is before anything goes wrong medically.

---

## Cost 2: The Diet That Isn't Working

This is the expensive one, and it does not show up as a line item at all.

Your cat is on a renal diet. Their phosphorus is still creeping up. So you go back to the vet. There is a consultation fee. There is a blood panel. Possibly a urine test. The vet reasonably concludes the diet is insufficient and escalates — a stricter formula, or a medication added.

None of that was the right response, because the diet was never being eaten as prescribed. Your cat was getting maybe 60% of their renal food and topping up from the regular bowl.

A single extra vet visit with bloodwork is commonly in the **$150–$400** range in the US depending on where you live and what is run. Two of those in a year, chasing a problem caused by cross-feeding, and the feeder has paid for itself with the wasted food still to come.

**The same pattern, in the conditions where it does the most damage:**

- **Diabetes.** A cat on a controlled carbohydrate diet who supplements from a free-fed bowl produces glucose readings that do not make sense, and insulin dosing gets adjusted against bad data. See [Managing a Diabetic Cat's Diet in a Multi-Cat Home](/blog/diabetic-cat-diet-multiple-cats).
- **Food elimination trials.** A trial requires near-total compliance for weeks. One contaminated meal and the weeks were wasted — that is the entire cost of the trial, repeated. See [Food Allergies in Multi-Cat Households](/blog/food-allergies-multiple-cats).
- **Weight management.** A cat on a measured portion who also eats their housemate's free-fed kibble does not lose weight, and the conclusion drawn is usually "this cat has a slow metabolism" rather than "this cat is eating more than we think." See [Helping an Overweight Cat Lose Weight](/blog/overweight-cat-diet-multiple-cats).
- **Kidney disease.** Restriction that is partial is close to no restriction at all. See [Feeding a Cat with Kidney Disease](/blog/kidney-diet-cat-multiple-cats).

## Cost 3: What You Are Doing Instead Right Now

Most people managing this without a feeder are using room separation. It is free in money and expensive in everything else.

**Count the meals.** Two separated meals a day is roughly 730 feeding events a year. Even at five minutes of corralling, waiting, and releasing, that is **60 hours a year**.

**Count the constraints.** You have to be home. Both meals. Every day. A late finish, an overnight away, a delayed train — each one is a missed dose of a medical diet. If someone else covers your cats, they have to execute the same routine correctly.

**Count the breakdown rate.** Systems requiring daily human discipline degrade. Not because people are careless, but because life happens. The plan that works in month one is often half-observed by month six, and nobody decided to stop.

Room separation is genuinely the right answer for some households — see [Do You Need a Separate Room to Feed Cats Differently?](/blog/separate-room-feed-cats-differently) for when. But "free" is not the correct word for it.

## When a Selective Feeder Is Not Worth It

There are real cases where the answer is no, and you should hear them before you buy.

**Nobody is on a medical diet and nobody is overweight.** If your cats eat the same food and maintain healthy weights, you are solving a problem you do not have. Buy a scale instead and keep an eye on things.

**The problem is speed, not access.** If one cat inhales their food and then stares at the other's bowl, but everyone is on the same diet and at a healthy weight, a slow feeder bowl costs a fraction as much. See [How to Stop a Cat from Eating Too Fast](/blog/stop-cat-eating-too-fast).

**You have one cat.** A selective feeder solves a multi-animal problem. With a single cat, a timed feeder does what you need.

**Your cat's diet change is temporary.** A two-week course of a recovery food usually does not justify the hardware. Separate rooms for two weeks is fine — it is the indefinite version that breaks down.

**The real problem is bullying, not diet.** If one cat physically drives another off their food, identification does not fix it. That needs a behavioural approach and better placement first. See [Cat Aggressive About Food](/blog/cat-aggressive-about-food-multi-cat-home).

## How to Decide in Five Minutes

1. Look up what you actually pay per month for the protected food.
2. Estimate honestly what share the wrong cat is getting. If you are unsure, it is higher than you think — see [7 Signs Your Cat Is Eating Another Cat's Food](/blog/signs-cat-eating-another-cats-food).
3. Multiply, then annualise.
4. Add one avoidable vet revisit if a medical diet is involved.
5. Compare against the setup cost.

For most households with one cat on a genuine prescription diet, the payback period lands inside a year. For households where nobody is on a medical diet, it usually does not — and that is a perfectly good reason not to buy one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much does a selective feeder actually cost?**
Standalone microchip feeders generally sit in the low hundreds of dollars per unit. Modular covers that work with your existing bowls cost less, and scale more cheaply across several cats. Prices move, so check current listings rather than trusting a number in a blog post.

**Do I need one feeder per cat?**
You need protection on each bowl whose contents matter. Often that is one cat's food, not everyone's. A common working setup is one protected bowl for the cat on the prescription diet and a normal bowl for the rest.

**Will it pay for itself if my cat is only on a weight-loss diet?**
Usually yes, but the saving is mostly not in food cost — it is in the diet actually working. Weight-loss plans fail far more often from uncounted calories than from the plan being wrong.

**Can I try room separation first and buy a feeder later?**
That is a sensible order, and many people arrive at a feeder exactly that way. Give separation an honest month. If you are still doing it correctly at every meal after four weeks, it may be a workable long-term system for you.

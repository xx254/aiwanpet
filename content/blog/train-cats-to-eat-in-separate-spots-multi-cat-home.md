You set down three bowls in three corners of the kitchen, step back, and watch. Within a minute the grey cat has finished hers and is standing over the tabby's bowl. The tabby has stopped eating to glare. The third cat, who was supposed to be by the fridge, is sitting under the table, looking at all of them and eating nothing. You did what the advice said and put the bowls apart. Nobody stayed where you put them.

If you are trying to train cats to eat in separate spots, this is the usual first week: a plan that makes sense on paper and cats that have not read it. This post covers why cats wander at mealtimes, how to build a routine where each cat has her own place, and what to do when the plan stalls.

---

## Why Cats Don't Stay at Their Own Bowl

### Cats are not wired to eat as a group

International Cat Care's guidance on [feeding your cat or kitten](https://icatcare.org/articles/feeding-your-cat-or-kitten) says that "cats generally do not like to share food bowls or be seen by other cats when eating," and that it is important to feed your cats in separate areas. Its page on [introducing cats](https://icatcare.org/articles/introducing-cats) describes cats as "naturally solitary when it comes to eating and playing."

That helps explain the wandering. A cat who eats in view of another cat is half-eating and half-watching. A cat who has finished early is not being rude when she checks the other bowls. She is a cat with an unfinished interest and a short walk.

### The spot may not feel safe

The same iCatCare page says each cat should have "a quiet, calm part of their home where they can eat out of sight of other cats," and that feeding areas should be away from litter trays, entry points to the home and busy areas. Cornell's Feline Health Center makes a related point in its [feeding guide](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/feeding-your-cat): cats can be choosy about where they eat, and the presence of other animals, dirty food containers or nearby litter boxes can put a cat off her food.

So when a cat will not stay where you put her, ask a boring question first: is it a spot she would choose? A bowl in a doorway, or in line of sight of the cat she avoids, may be one she abandons.

### Nobody told them which spot is whose

In a house where bowls have always gone wherever there was room, no spot belongs to any cat. If the grey cat eats at the window on Monday, the hallway on Tuesday and the kitchen on Wednesday, "the window" does not mean "mine" to her. Places are learned through repetition, not through your intentions.

## Before You Train: Fix the Layout

Training rarely works against a bad layout, so start with the geography.

### Give each cat a spot out of sight of the others

The aim is a visual break, not just distance. A bowl ten feet from another bowl with a clear line between them is still a shared table. Put one in a different room, or behind a door, a sofa or a piece of furniture. If space is short, [feeding multiple cats in a small apartment](/blog/feed-multiple-cats-small-apartment) has layouts that work without a spare room, and [how many feeding stations you need](/blog/how-many-feeding-stations-for-multiple-cats) explains the thinking behind more than one station per cat.

International Cat Care's feeding page says that, ideally, there should be 2 or 3 feeding and watering areas for each cat in your home. For most households that is a counsel of perfection, but it shows the direction to lean: more places, spread out.

### Choose a spot the cat already likes

Watch where each cat naturally goes at mealtimes for a few days. A nervous cat often prefers a corner with her back to a wall. A confident cat will eat almost anywhere. Give the nervous cat her preferred spot first.

### Decide what "done" looks like

Settle the rule you will enforce. For example: each cat eats at her own station, the bowl is picked up once she walks away, and nobody eats from a second bowl. Write it down if more than one person feeds the cats. Training collapses when one adult follows the rule and another does not.

## How to Teach Each Cat Her Own Spot

This is a practical routine, not a research protocol. The sources above support separate feeding areas and quiet spots; the steps below are common sense built on that. Adjust them to your cats.

### Step 1: Same times, same places

Consistency does most of the work. Put meals down at predictable times and always put each cat's food in the same place. If you have not set a rhythm yet, [the best feeding schedule for multiple cats](/blog/best-feeding-schedule-multiple-cats) is a good starting point.

### Step 2: Walk each cat to her spot

Do not expect the cats to find their places on day one. Put the bowl down at the station, and encourage the cat towards it: call her, or set the bowl down while she is watching. Let her eat. Do not hover.

If a cat arrives at the wrong bowl, calmly move her back to her own. Avoid shouting or chasing; mealtimes should not become the stressful part of the day. If they already are, [signs of mealtime stress in a multi-cat home](/blog/cat-mealtime-stress-signs-multi-cat-home) is worth reading.

### Step 3: Stay present, at a distance

For the first week or two, be in the room, close enough to step in. Your job is to interrupt before a cat reaches another cat's bowl, not after the argument. Over time you can move further away, then leave the room for part of the meal.

### Step 4: Use a door if you can

A closed door is the most reliable boundary a cat will ever respect. If one cat is a persistent raider, feed her behind a door for her meal and let her out when everyone has finished. Not every home can do this, and [do you need a separate room to feed cats differently](/blog/separate-room-feed-cats-differently) looks at the alternatives.

### Step 5: Remove the bowl when the meal is over

Leaving food down turns the station into a place to check, and it invites quick eaters to move along the line. Pick up the bowl, or the uneaten food, after a reasonable time. For wet food, see [how long wet cat food can sit out](/blog/how-long-can-wet-cat-food-sit-out-multi-cat-home).

### Step 6: Move bowls slowly

If the ideal spot is across the house, move the bowl a short way every few days. If a cat stops eating as you move things, go back a step. A cat who is not eating is a more urgent problem than a cat who eats in the wrong room, and [my cat isn't eating](/blog/cat-not-eating-multi-cat-home) explains how to work out which cat skipped.

## A Hypothetical Week

Picture a three-cat flat. Pepper is quick and eats first. Olive is slow and nervous. Tuck goes wherever the food is. The owner has been feeding all three in a row along the kitchen wall.

On Monday she puts Olive's bowl in the bedroom with the door ajar, Tuck's in the hallway, and Pepper's in the kitchen. Pepper finishes in a minute and strolls down the hall to Tuck's bowl. The owner, sitting on the hallway floor, carries her back. This happens four times on Monday, twice on Tuesday and once on Thursday. By the second week she can leave the hall and Pepper mostly stays put. The scenario is invented, but the pattern is the realistic one: lots of calm redirection at first, then less.

## When This Won't Work

- **Training does not make a food thief honest.** A very food-motivated cat will keep trying other bowls whenever she can. Training reduces it; it does not remove it.
- **It needs a layout that allows distance.** In a small home there may be nowhere to put a bowl out of sight. Doors, furniture or staggered mealtimes can help, but they take effort.
- **It depends on you being there.** If the household is empty at mealtimes, the rule cannot be enforced in person.
- **It does not solve diet differences.** If one cat is on a prescription diet, a raid on her bowl is a health matter, not just manners. Ask your vet, and see [how to feed multiple cats separately](/blog/how-to-feed-multiple-cats-separately).
- **Some cats refuse to move.** A cat who stops eating when you change her spot needs a slower approach, or a vet check if it continues.

## FAQ

### How long does it take to train cats to eat in separate places?

There is no reliable number. It varies by cat and by how often the rule slips. Expect a couple of weeks of consistent redirection, longer for very food-motivated cats. If a cat stops eating during the change, slow down and consult your vet.

### Should I feed my cats in separate rooms?

It is often the simplest approach. International Cat Care advises feeding cats in separate areas, with a quiet spot where each cat can eat out of sight of the others. A separate room is one way to do that, but a screened corner can also work.

### What if one cat keeps eating the other cat's food?

Stay with the cats at mealtimes, redirect the raider calmly, feed her behind a door if you can, and pick up bowls as soon as each cat is done. See [why your cat steals the other cat's food](/blog/cat-stealing-other-cats-food) for the reasons behind it.

### Do I need to train my cats if they already get along?

Not always. Cats that eat side by side without trouble may not need a change. But International Cat Care notes that cats generally do not like to be seen eating by other cats, so watching for signs of tension is still worthwhile.

You put down a bowl of kitten food for the pregnant cat, the one who turned up thin on the back step and is now visibly round. She eats a few mouthfuls and wanders off to lie in the laundry basket. Ten minutes later you walk past and the bowl is licked clean. Your neutered tabby is sitting next to it, washing his face, looking pleased with himself.

Feeding a pregnant or nursing cat when you have other cats is a strange problem. For once, the vet advice is to feed one cat *more*: richer food, more often, sometimes left out all day. Every other cat in the house hears that as good news. The cat who needs the calories eats in small amounts and walks away. The cats who don't need them get to the bowl first.

This guide covers what a pregnant and nursing cat actually needs to eat, stage by stage, and how to get it to her and not to everyone else.

---

## What a Pregnant Cat Needs to Eat

### Switch to kitten food early

International Cat Care's guide to [cat pregnancy](https://icatcare.org/articles/cat-pregnancy) says: "As soon as you're aware of your cat's pregnancy, we recommend feeding your cat a growth type diet." It adds that this "can continue to be fed after the birth of the kittens and until they're weaned."

VCA Animal Hospitals' article on [feeding the pregnant cat](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/feeding-the-pregnant-cat) agrees: "High-quality commercial kitten food or growth formulation is recommended during feline pregnancy." VCA notes that many vets recommend diets that have passed an AAFCO feeding trial for gestation/lactation or for all life stages. That wording will be on the label.

That's the first multi-cat problem. Kitten food is richer than adult food, and your other cats will notice. Our guide to [feeding cats at different life stages in one home](/blog/feeding-cats-different-life-stages) explains what happens when adults keep eating kitten food.

### Don't overdo it in the first weeks

Pregnancy is short. The [Merck Veterinary Manual](https://www.merckvetmanual.com/cat-owners/reproductive-disorders-of-cats/management-of-reproduction-of-cats) says it "lasts 60 to 65 days." For the first two trimesters, VCA says, "the mother's nutritional requirements are the same as those for a young adult cat." She shouldn't lose weight or condition, but she doesn't need to be stuffed either.

VCA is blunt about the risk: "Obesity is the most common nutrition-related problem in pregnant cats," and over-feeding can lead to "difficult or prolonged labor." It recommends meal feeding during pregnancy as "the best way to control body condition and weight gain."

### Increase food in the last three weeks

Merck says "she will need 25% more food during the last 3 weeks of her pregnancy." VCA adds that "feeding multiple small meals may help the mother to maintain adequate nutrient and calorie intake," especially in the final trimester.

### Skip the supplements

Merck says plainly: "do not give her any dietary supplements (such as calcium or vitamins)." It links calcium supplements in pregnancy to a higher risk of low blood calcium after the birth. If you're unsure, ask your vet before adding anything.

## What a Nursing Cat Needs to Eat

### A lot more, and often

VCA's article on [feeding the nursing cat](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/feeding-the-nursing-cat) says: "Nursing cats have the greatest energy requirements of any other life stage." Merck puts a number on it: "While nursing, she will need 2 to 3 times more food than normal."

She can't fit that into two meals. VCA suggests a food "with a very high energy density so the cat can take in more calories with less volume." It also recommends leaving food out so she can "snack and graze continuously," or else offering small, frequent meals.

### Free-choice, with one exception

VCA's pregnancy article says "free-choice feeding during the first 3 to 4 weeks of lactation provides several advantages." She eats on her own schedule, and the kittens can sample solid food as soon as they're ready, at about three weeks.

But VCA also says "free-choice feeding is not advised for a mother nursing only one or two kittens," because of the risk of mastitis. With a small litter, stick to measured meals and ask your vet how much.

### Her needs peak later than you'd think

VCA says "peak milk production occurs around 3 to 4 weeks of lactation," but "the mother cat's energy requirement peaks at six weeks post-partum." So don't start cutting back just because the kittens look big. They're sharing her bowl by then, so a bowl that empties fast doesn't mean she's had enough.

iCatCare's guide to [bringing up a litter of kittens](https://icatcare.org/articles/bringing-up-a-litter-of-kittens-health-considerations) says weaning "should begin at around three to four weeks of age," and kittens should be "fully weaned by around six to eight weeks of age." VCA says that as weaning progresses, "the mother's calorie requirement will begin to decline," and once it's done she can go back to her usual intake.

### Measure, don't guess

VCA suggests measuring what you put out at the start of the day, adding up "any top-up amounts added throughout the day," and measuring what's left at the end. In a single-cat home that tells you what she ate. In a multi-cat home it only tells you what *disappeared*. If your other cats can reach the bowl, the number means nothing.

## Why This Is Harder With Other Cats

Think of her bowl as a buffet that never closes. During pregnancy and nursing, the advice pushes you towards exactly the setup that struggles in multi-cat homes: rich food, left out, eaten slowly. Our comparison of [free feeding vs scheduled feeding for multiple cats](/blog/free-feeding-vs-scheduled-feeding-multiple-cats) explains why open bowls go to whoever is fastest.

Three things can go wrong at once:

- **She doesn't get enough.** She eats a little and leaves, and another cat finishes it. This matters most in late pregnancy and in the weeks after the birth.
- **Your other cats gain weight.** Weeks of stolen kitten food add up. If one of them is already on a diet, see our guide to [helping an overweight cat lose weight in a multi-cat home](/blog/overweight-cat-diet-multiple-cats).
- **You lose track.** You can't tell if she's eating less because she's unwell or because someone else is eating her share.

## How to Feed Her Separately

### Give her a room

The simplest answer for most homes is a nesting room. iCatCare says she "will look for a quiet, safe, and often dark place to have her kittens," usually in the final two weeks of pregnancy. A spare room or bathroom with a box, bedding, water, a litter tray and her food covers both needs. Close the door and the other cats can't reach her bowl.

Our article on whether [you need a separate room to feed cats differently](/blog/separate-room-feed-cats-differently) covers how to make a room setup work day to day.

### Feed everyone else on a schedule

If her food is left out, everyone else's shouldn't be. Feeding the other cats at set times in another part of the house gives them less reason to go hunting for hers. It also makes it obvious when someone skips a meal because they found a better one. If someone keeps slipping through the door, the signs in our guide to [why a cat steals the other cat's food](/blog/cat-stealing-other-cats-food) will help you spot who.

### Plan for when the kittens start eating

From about three to four weeks, the kittens will start eating solids, often from her bowl. That's expected. It's also when kittens start wandering, so the door gets harder to keep shut. Put a shallow kitten dish in the nesting room so they have their own food, and keep measuring what goes in and what's left.

## Picture a Typical Few Months

Picture a two-cat flat. Pepper, the resident, is a neutered adult on measured meals. Mabel arrives pregnant.

**Most of the pregnancy:** Mabel moves to kitten food in measured meals, fed in the spare room with the door shut. Pepper eats his usual food in the kitchen at the same time.

**The last three weeks:** Mabel's portion goes up by about a quarter, split into smaller, more frequent meals. She starts sleeping in the box in the spare room.

**The first weeks after the birth:** She has five kittens, so food stays out all day in the spare room. Pepper tries the door every time someone opens it.

**Weaning:** The kittens are eating solids and exploring. Mabel's food keeps disappearing faster, and it's harder to tell who's eating it. This is the stretch where a closed door stops being enough.

## When This Won't Work

None of this replaces a vet. If she stops eating, loses weight, or seems unwell during pregnancy or after the birth, call your vet promptly. Rationing food won't fix a medical problem.

A separate room also isn't an option in every home. In a studio flat, with a cat who panics when shut in, or with other cats who scratch at the door all night, you'll need another way to keep her food separate. And once the kittens are weaned, you'll need a plan to move her back to adult food and normal portions. Our guide to [switching cat food in a multi-cat home](/blog/switch-cat-food-multiple-cats) walks through that.

## FAQ

### Can my other cats eat the pregnant cat's kitten food?

It's not meant for them. VCA and iCatCare recommend a growth or kitten diet for the pregnant and nursing cat, not the rest of the house, and weeks of extra rich food can add weight to cats who don't need it. Feed them separately where you can.

### How much should I feed a pregnant cat?

During the first two trimesters, VCA says her needs are about the same as a young adult cat's. Merck says she needs about 25% more food in the last three weeks of pregnancy. Watch her weight and body condition, and ask your vet for a target.

### Should I leave food out all the time for a nursing cat?

Often, yes. VCA says free-choice feeding in the first three to four weeks of lactation has several advantages, but does not advise it for a mother nursing only one or two kittens because of the risk of mastitis. Merck says she may need 2 to 3 times more food than normal while nursing.

### When can a nursing cat go back to adult food?

iCatCare says she can stay on a growth diet until the kittens are weaned, usually by six to eight weeks. VCA says her calorie needs drop as weaning progresses, and once it's complete she can return to her usual intake and move to adult food.

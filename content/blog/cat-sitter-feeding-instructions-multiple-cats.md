The note on the fridge says "feed the cats, wet food in the morning, dry at night." You read it back at the airport and realise it says almost nothing. Which cat gets the kidney food? Which one needs the pill hidden in a treat? Who eats fast and will clear the other two bowls if nobody stands there? The sitter has never met your cats, and a cat sitter's first visit is a lot of strange faces and a lot of bowls.

Writing cat sitter feeding instructions for one cat is easy. Writing them for a multi-cat home is a different job, because most of what goes wrong is about *which cat ate what*, not whether food appeared. This guide covers what to write down, how to set up the home so the notes are easy to follow, and how to help the sitter notice a skipped meal.

---

## Why Multi-Cat Feeding Is Harder for a Sitter

You know your cats by sight, by sound and by the way they walk into the kitchen. A sitter on a 30-minute visit knows none of that. Two black cats look the same from across the room. A cat that hides when a stranger arrives may not come out to eat at all, and the sitter may not notice that only two of three bowls were touched.

Meanwhile the usual household workarounds, like you standing between the fast eater and the slow one, disappear. If your normal system depends on you being there, it needs to be turned into something written down or something physical before you go.

There's also the cats' side of it. Sure Petcare's [advice for cat sitters](https://www.surepetcare.com/advice-news/cat-care/seasonal-cat-care/cat-sitter-prepare-for-looking-after-cat) says to keep "the cat's food bowl in its usual location" so the cat isn't upset by changes, and to "make sure you know how often and when to feed them." Your goal is to change as little as possible except the person holding the scoop.

## What to Write Down for Each Cat

Don't write one note for the household. Write one short block per cat, with a photo of the cat next to it. A photo beats a description ("grey tabby, medium") every time.

### The basics, per cat

- **Name, photo and one identifying feature** (white sock on the left paw, notched ear, the collar colour).
- **What it eats:** exact food, wet or dry, and the amount in a unit the sitter can measure (one scoop, half a can, 40 g on the kitchen scale). "A bit" is not an amount.
- **When it eats:** the times, as close to your usual routine as the sitter's visits allow.
- **Where it eats:** the bowl, the spot, the room. Keep this the same as at home.
- **What it won't eat,** and what it eats too fast.

### The cats who need more than a scoop

Any cat on a special diet gets a bold line at the top of its block. That's the cat whose food the others must not touch. If you're managing prescription food, our guide to [feeding prescription cat food in a multi-cat home](/blog/prescription-cat-food-multiple-cats) explains why one stolen meal matters more than it looks.

For a cat with a medical condition, Sure Petcare suggests the sitter "find out what medication they will need and ask the owner to demonstrate how to administer the medicine if possible." Do a demonstration visit if you can. If a cat is on medication, our post on [medicating a cat in a multi-cat home](/blog/medicating-cat-multi-cat-home) covers how to keep the wrong cat from getting the dose.

### The "leave extra" rule

Sure Petcare also advises leaving food "plus a bit extra in case the owner is delayed coming home." Flights get cancelled. Put an extra few days of every cat's food in a clearly labelled spot, separated by cat, so the sitter doesn't have to guess.

### Vet and emergency details

Sure Petcare says to leave "details of their pet's vet so that you know who to contact" if a cat becomes unwell or has an accident. Add your own phone number, the vet's number, the nearest emergency clinic, and written permission for the sitter to authorise a vet visit if you can't be reached.

## Set Up the Home Before You Leave

Notes only work if the house cooperates. Spend an evening or two on this a week before you travel.

### Do a trial run with the sitter

A meet-and-greet before you go matters. Sure Petcare recommends the sitter "meet the cat and owner before the owner goes away" to get a sense of each cat's temperament. In a multi-cat home, use that visit to do one actual feeding together, so the sitter sees who rushes in first, who waits, and where the shy one eats.

### Keep the routine, change nothing else

This is a bad week for new food, new bowls or a new feeding spot. If you're thinking of switching foods, finish the transition well before you go. Our guide on [how to switch cat food in a multi-cat home](/blog/switch-cat-food-multiple-cats) explains why the switch should be slow, and a trip is the wrong time to be mid-switch.

### Decide how the cats will be separated

Think through the actual choreography of a visit:

- **Separate rooms with closed doors.** The sitter feeds each cat in its room and picks up the bowls after. This works well, but it needs clear door instructions, and the sitter has to remember to let everyone out again.
- **Feeding in a row, supervised.** Fine for two easy-going cats on the same food. Harder for three.
- **Locking down the special-diet cat's food.** Whether it's a closed door, a cupboard or a physical barrier over the bowl, the cat on a special diet shouldn't depend on the sitter standing guard.

If your cats already steal from each other, the sitter will not catch it. Our post on [why cats steal each other's food](/blog/cat-stealing-other-cats-food) describes how quickly that habit sets in, which is a good reason to build the barrier into the setup rather than into the instructions.

## Help the Sitter Spot a Skipped Meal

Of everything on the list, this one is the most important, and the one that gets left off.

Cornell's Feline Health Center explains that when a cat stops eating, fat is mobilised and can build up in the liver, a condition called [hepatic lipidosis](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/hepatic-lipidosis). Cornell says it "can occur in cats that otherwise appear to be normal and healthy as well as in those that are seriously overweight," and that it is usually secondary to some other underlying problem. The University of Illinois College of Veterinary Medicine says the key warning sign is "not eating for multiple days," and that "overweight or obese cats have a greater chance of developing hepatic lipidosis" ([Illinois vet med column](https://vetmed.illinois.edu/pet-health-columns/hepatic-lipidosis-when-cats-dont-eat/)).

Neither source gives an exact number of hours, so don't invent one in your note. What you can do is give the sitter a clear rule: **if a named cat leaves its food untouched, message me the same day, and if it stays untouched across visits, call the vet.**

To make that possible:

- Ask the sitter to send a short message after every visit: which cats were seen, which bowls were empty, which were not.
- Tell the sitter to count heads. Three cats means three cats seen, not "the cats are fine".
- Tell them what a normal eating pattern looks like for each cat. A cat that usually inhales its food and now leaves half is giving information.

If you're working out how to tell *which* cat skipped a meal from the empty bowls alone, our post on [what to do when a cat isn't eating in a multi-cat home](/blog/cat-not-eating-multi-cat-home) goes through it.

## A Sample Block You Can Copy

Picture a three-cat home: Mabel, a senior on a kidney diet; Otis, a young cat who eats anything and everything; and Fig, who hides when strangers visit. This is how the note might read for Mabel (this is a made-up example, so adapt the details to your cats):

> **MABEL** (grey, white chin, blue collar). Kidney food only, from the labelled tin in the cupboard. Half a tin morning and evening, in her bowl in the spare room. Close the door while she eats. Do NOT let Otis in. Pick up the bowl after 30 minutes. If she leaves more than half, message me.

Notice what it does: it names the cat, shows how to recognise her, gives the food, amount, place, door rule, and what to do if something looks off. Make one of these for each cat, and put them where the sitter will see them first.

## When This Won't Work

Even a great note can't solve some problems. A sitter on a short visit can't watch a slow eater for 30 minutes, and can't stop a determined cat from getting into a room. A hiding cat may not come out for a stranger however well you prepare.

For some homes, the best answer is not a drop-in visit at all but a live-in sitter, or boarding for the cat with the most complex needs. A cat with a serious medical condition, or one who needs several medications, may need a person with more experience than a friendly neighbour. And any physical set-up, from a closed door to a feeder, should be tested before you leave, not on the morning of your flight.

## FAQ

### How often should a cat sitter visit a multi-cat home?

There's no single right answer, and I couldn't find an authoritative number to quote. The practical rule is that the more cats you have, and the more of them need special food or medication, the more the visit schedule should match your normal feeding times. Talk it through with the sitter and your vet.

### What should I tell the sitter about each cat's food?

The food, the amount in a measurable unit, the time, the location, and whether the cat should be kept apart from the others. Add a photo of the cat. Sure Petcare also recommends leaving a little extra food in case you're delayed getting home.

### How do I stop the sitter from feeding the wrong cat the wrong food?

Reduce what the sitter has to remember. Label food by cat, use photos, separate special-diet cats physically, and ask for a message after each visit that names every cat seen. A physical barrier is more reliable than a note.

### What if one of my cats stops eating while I'm away?

Ask the sitter to tell you the same day. Cornell's Feline Health Center says a cat that stops eating can develop hepatic lipidosis, and the University of Illinois names "not eating for multiple days" as the key warning sign. Give the sitter your vet's details and permission to arrange care.

Picture the sink at 7 a.m. Three cat bowls from last night, a fourth with a dried crust of wet food welded to the rim, a scoop with kibble dust on the handle, and a water dish you cannot remember filling. One cat is already complaining behind you. You rinse the worst of it, wipe a finger around the edge, and put everything back down.

If that is your morning, you are not careless. Learning how to clean cat food bowls in a multi-cat home is mostly a question of volume: more cats means more bowls, more leftovers, and more chances to skip a step. This post covers what the FDA and CDC actually recommend, how to build a routine that fits several cats, and the parts people forget.

## How Often Should You Wash Cat Food Bowls?

The two U.S. agencies that publish guidance give slightly different answers, and both are useful.

The FDA's page on [safe handling of pet food and treats](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/animal-health-literacy/tips-safe-handling-pet-food-and-treats) says to wash pet food bowls and scooping and measuring utensils with soap and hot water after each use. Its page on [proper storage of pet food](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/animal-health-literacy/proper-storage-pet-food-treats) says to wash and dry bowls after each use, and to wash water bowls daily.

The CDC's page on [cleaning and disinfecting pet supplies](https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-pets/about/cleaning-and-disinfecting-pet-supplies.html) is more specific about type of food. It says to clean pet bowls after every use for wet food, and every day for dry food and water. Its rule of thumb is to clean pet items as often as your own.

### A simple way to read both

- **Wet food bowls:** wash after every meal. Both agencies agree here.
- **Dry food bowls:** the CDC says daily. The FDA's wording is "after each use", which is stricter. If you top up a bowl without emptying it, you are not really washing it between uses, so empty and wash it on a fixed day-by-day schedule.
- **Water bowls:** daily, according to both.

None of this requires a deep clean. The CDC says to wash with soap or detergent and warm water. The FDA says hot. Either way, the point is soap, water and a bit of scrubbing, then drying.

## Why Multi-Cat Homes Make This Harder

### More bowls, more small decisions

With one cat you wash one bowl twice a day. With four cats on different diets you may have six or eight dishes, two scoops, an open can with a lid, and a feeder. Nobody decides to stop washing them. The routine just slips.

### Cats who graze leave food that sits

A bowl that is topped up all day is never really empty, so it never gets washed. If this describes your house, [free feeding vs scheduled feeding for multiple cats](/blog/free-feeding-vs-scheduled-feeding-multiple-cats) explains why set meals also make cleaning easier: the bowl is empty at a known time, which is when you wash it.

### Leftovers pile up

The FDA says to promptly refrigerate or throw out unused or leftover canned and pouched pet food, and to tightly cover food you refrigerate. For how long wet food can stay in a bowl, see [how long wet cat food can sit out](/blog/how-long-can-wet-cat-food-sit-out-multi-cat-home). A bowl that held wet food and sat for a few hours is the one that most needs washing, not rinsing.

## A Routine That Works for Several Cats

Think of it like a restaurant kitchen: nobody washes each plate when the customer finishes, but there is a point in the day when the whole stack gets done. You want the same "closing time" for your bowls.

### 1. Pick up and tip out at a set time

Whatever the cats left, throw it away or, for canned food, cover and refrigerate it. The FDA suggests disposing of old or spoiled food in a securely tied plastic bag in a covered trash can.

### 2. Wash after the last meal of the day

If you feed at, say, breakfast and dinner, wash everything once after dinner and once after breakfast for wet food. Dry-food bowls can follow the CDC's daily schedule.

### 3. Use a dishwasher if the bowls allow it

The CDC says pet bowls can be washed in the sink or the dishwasher if labelled dishwasher-safe, on any wash setting, and that you should use the high-temperature or sanitizing setting to disinfect. Running all the bowls in one load is the easiest fix for a multi-cat home, because the whole stack gets cleaned at once. Owning two sets of bowls means one set is always clean and dry while the other is in the wash.

### 4. Keep the scoop separate

The FDA says not to use your pet's food bowl as a scoop, and to use a clean scoop, spoon or cup only for pet food. In a house with several diets, that also stops one food's crumbs from ending up in another cat's bowl. If you feed prescription food, [how to feed prescription cat food in a multi-cat home](/blog/prescription-cat-food-multiple-cats) covers keeping each cat's food separate.

### 5. Wash your hands

The FDA says to wash your hands for at least 20 seconds with soap and hot water before and after handling pet food and treats. It is easy to skip when you are feeding in a rush.

## The Parts People Forget

### The storage container

The FDA says to wash and dry the storage container between finishing one bag of kibble and filling it with another, to get residual fat and crumbs off the surfaces. It also says to make sure it has a lid that fits snugly and to keep food in its original bag or container. If you keep several diets in a house, [how to store cat food in a multi-cat home](/blog/store-cat-food-multi-cat-home) goes through the options.

### The water bowl

Water bowls are easy to top up without washing. Both agencies say daily.

### The feeder or mat

Anything the food touches counts, including a feeder tray or the mat under the bowl. The CDC and FDA pages do not list these by name. Applying the same rule (wash, rinse, dry) is sensible, but check the manufacturer's care instructions first, since some parts will not go in a dishwasher.

### The sink

The CDC notes that washing pet items in the kitchen sink may spread germs to your food. Rinse the sink afterwards, and keep sponges for pet dishes apart from your own, if you can.

## What Bowl Material Does to Cleaning

International Cat Care's page on [feeding your cat or kitten](https://icatcare.org/articles/feeding-your-cat-or-kitten) says plastic bowls can pick up odours that cats do not like, and recommends glass or ceramic. That page also says cats prefer shallow bowls or saucers. Both points make washing easier: a shallow glass or ceramic dish is quick to scrub and does not hold smells between washes. For the full discussion of shapes and materials, see [the best cat food bowl for a multi-cat home](/blog/best-cat-food-bowls-multi-cat-home).

The same page says cats generally do not like to share food bowls or be seen by other cats while eating. Separate bowls mean more washing, but that is the trade-off for a calmer mealtime. [Do cats prefer to eat alone?](/blog/do-cats-prefer-to-eat-alone-multi-cat-home) looks at why.

## Disinfecting: When Soap Is Not Enough

For everyday cat food, the guidance above is soap and water. The CDC adds a disinfecting step for when you need one: a diluted bleach solution of 1/4 cup (2 oz) bleach to 1 gallon of water, with the item soaked for at least 10 minutes. It warns never to mix bleach with other cleaners such as ammonia, because this can produce dangerous gases, and to store disinfectants out of the reach of pets. It also says there is currently not enough research on how well vinegar cleans or disinfects, so it does not recommend relying on it.

If your vet has told you a cat has an illness, ask your vet before choosing a method.

A feeder with a lid adds one question: what needs washing and what should stay dry? The answer depends on the model, so follow the manufacturer's care instructions rather than guessing. ## When This Won't Work

- **You cannot wash after every meal.** If you work long hours, a daily wash is a realistic target. [Feeding multiple cats when you work long hours](/blog/feeding-multiple-cats-when-you-work-long-hours) has ideas for making the day easier.
- **Cats who eat from a bowl all day.** Without set meals, there is no natural moment to wash. Swapping in a clean bowl for the dirty one is the workaround.
- **Dishwasher-unsafe bowls.** Some bowls and feeder parts cannot go in the machine. Hand-wash them, and check the care label.
- **A shield does not clean itself.** It protects a portion from other cats. It does not remove the need to wash the bowl underneath.
- **Medical situations.** If a cat is unwell or you are managing infection, your vet's advice beats general guidance.

## FAQ

### How often should I wash my cat's food bowl?

The CDC says to clean pet bowls after every use for wet food and every day for dry food and water. The FDA says to wash food bowls after each use and water bowls daily.

### Can I put cat bowls in the dishwasher?

The CDC says pet bowls can be washed in the dishwasher if labelled dishwasher-safe. Use any wash setting, and the high-temperature or sanitizing setting to disinfect.

### Is it okay to wash cat bowls in the kitchen sink?

The CDC says you can wash dog and cat bowls in the kitchen sink, but cautions that doing so may spread germs to your food. Rinse the sink afterwards.

### What material is best for a cat food bowl?

International Cat Care recommends glass or ceramic over plastic, because plastic bowls can pick up odours cats dislike. It also says cats prefer shallow bowls or saucers.

### Do I need to wash my hands after feeding the cats?

The FDA says to wash your hands for at least 20 seconds with soap and hot water before and after handling pet food and treats.

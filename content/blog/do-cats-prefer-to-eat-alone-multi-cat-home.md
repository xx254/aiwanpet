You put down two bowls, side by side on the same mat, and call the cats. The big tabby arrives first and starts eating. The small grey cat sits two feet away, watching, then edges forward, then backs off when the tabby lifts his head. Eventually she wanders off and the tabby finishes both bowls. You were sure that was fair. Two bowls, two cats, everyone fed.

If you have ever wondered whether cats prefer to eat alone, this scene is part of the answer. A pair of bowls next to each other feels like a shared dinner table to us. To a cat, it can feel more like being asked to eat next to a rival. This post explains what vets say about solitary eating, why bowl spacing matters more than bowl count, and what to do when space and time are limited.

---

## Do Cats Prefer to Eat Alone?

For most cats, yes. A 2018 feeding-programs paper from the American Association of Feline Practitioners panel, published in the *Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery*, says: "Cats are solitary predators that consume small prey, and they prefer to eat often and alone" ([Sadek et al., Feline feeding programs](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11343346)). The same paper notes that cats can naturally spend half of every 24 hours looking for and obtaining food.

Keep that picture in mind. A wild-living cat hunts alone, eats what it catches, and moves on. Our pet cats have eaten from bowls for a long time, but their wiring around food has not been rewritten to suit the dinner table.

That does not mean every cat in your house is miserable at shared mealtimes. Some bonded pairs happily eat near each other. But "eats alone" is the default, and a good reason to be suspicious when a mealtime that "should" be easy keeps going wrong.

## Why Side-by-Side Bowls Don't Give Each Cat Its Own Space

Think of a restaurant that gives you your own table versus one that seats you on a long shared bench beside a stranger whose elbow keeps touching yours. The food is the same. The experience is not.

Two bowls on one mat is the shared bench. Both cats can see and smell each other and judge exactly how close the other one is. The paper above says "competition for food can cause conflict and pre-feeding aggression," and that forcing a cat to eat close to another cat it otherwise chooses to avoid creates anxiety, stress and health problems.

The RSPCA makes the same point from the resource side. It says competition or conflict over shared resources can cause significant stress in multi-cat households. Its rule of thumb is one resource for each cat plus one extra, in separate locations. For two cats it suggests at least three food bowls, three water bowls and three litter trays, all in physically separated locations, with food kept out of sight of the other cats' bowls ([RSPCA Knowledgebase](https://kb.rspca.org.au/knowledge-base/what-do-i-need-to-know-about-multi-cat-households/)).

The key word is *separated*. Two bowls inches apart count as one feeding spot in a cat's mind, however many dishes are on the floor.

## What Mealtime Stress Looks Like

Not every uncomfortable cat will hiss or swat. The 2018 paper warns that cats may not show obvious signs of tension. In practice the signs are often quiet:

- A cat who eats a few mouthfuls, then leaves, and comes back later.
- A cat who waits at a distance until the others are done.
- A cat who bolts food when another cat is nearby.
- A cat who stares at another cat's bowl instead of eating their own.

The AAFP statement warns that anxious cats, "in an attempt to avoid other pets in the household, may not access the food frequently enough and lose weight" ([AAFP, Feline Feeding Programs](https://www.catvets.com/guidelines/practice-guidelines/how-to-feed)). The cat losing out is easy to miss because they are not the one making noise. If you are not sure who is actually eating, our post on [which cat skipped a meal](/blog/cat-not-eating-multi-cat-home) shows how to find out.

Meanwhile the confident cat can eat more than their share. The dvm360 write-up of the AAFP statement notes that pets in multi-cat households often receive "either large meals or an ad libitum food supply," which it links to overeating ([dvm360](https://www.dvm360.com/view/aafp-releases-consensus-statement-on-feline-feeding)). The same set-up can leave one cat underfed and another overweight.

## How to Give Each Cat Its Own Eating Space

You do not need a spare room. What matters is that each cat can eat without watching, or being watched by, another cat. dvm360 reports the AAFP advice that owners of multiple cats "should always provide separate feeding areas for each cat." Here are ways to get there, from most to least space-hungry.

### Separate rooms

Feed one cat in the kitchen, one in the bedroom, one in the hallway with a door closed. Our guide to [feeding multiple cats separately](/blog/how-to-feed-multiple-cats-separately) covers doors, timing and cats who finish early.

### Distance and visual breaks

If you cannot give every cat a room, put the bowls as far apart as the home allows and block the line of sight. A bookcase, a sofa back, or a corner can work. Aim for what the RSPCA describes: food out of sight of other cats' bowls, and cats not facing each other while they eat.

### Different times

Staggering meals gives each cat a quiet turn and needs no extra furniture, just a free pair of hands. [Free feeding vs scheduled feeding](/blog/free-feeding-vs-scheduled-feeding-multiple-cats) compares the two approaches.

### In a small flat

Small homes are the hardest. [Feeding multiple cats in a small apartment](/blog/feed-multiple-cats-small-apartment) works through what you can do with what you have, and [whether you need a separate room at all](/blog/separate-room-feed-cats-differently) looks at the trade-offs.

## A Hypothetical Two-Cat Flat

Picture a one-bedroom flat with two cats, Biscuit and Juno. Biscuit is bold and fast. Juno is thin, a little nervous, and hangs back. At the moment the bowls sit side by side next to the fridge.

A small change: Biscuit's bowl moves to the kitchen, on the far side of the counter. Juno's goes in the bedroom, near the wardrobe where she naps. With a door or a few metres between them, they cannot see each other eat. A second water bowl goes in the hallway. The food has not changed, but each cat now eats without an audience.

This is a made-up example, so adjust it to your own layout. The principle is distance plus a break in the line of sight.

## Where Does the Fast Cat's Food Go?

Separate spots help the shy cat, but they do not stop the bold cat strolling over to the shy cat's bowl once she steps away. That is the next problem most owners hit. [Why cats steal each other's food](/blog/cat-stealing-other-cats-food) explains why, and [what to do when a cat is aggressive about food](/blog/cat-aggressive-about-food-multi-cat-home) covers harder cases.

Some owners supervise each meal and lift the bowl after a set time. Others use a feeder that physically guards the food. The AAFP statement lists "in some instances, automatic feeders" among the tools in a feeding programme, alongside puzzle feeders and forage feeding. [Do microchip cat feeders actually work](/blog/do-microchip-cat-feeders-work) looks at how the RFID kind compares. Either way, a feeder is a second step after you have given the cats enough space, not a substitute for it.

## When This Won't Work

Separate feeding spaces are a good default, not magic.

- **Some bonded cats do fine together.** If two cats have always eaten side by side without tension, you may not need to change anything. Watch for the quiet signs first.
- **A cat leaving its bowl is not always stress.** Appetite changes can be medical. If a cat eats less or loses weight, see your vet rather than assuming it is placement.
- **Separation cannot fix guarding alone.** If a cat is attacking others at mealtimes, talk to your vet or a qualified behaviourist.
- **Small homes limit what is possible.** You may only get a few metres of separation. That is still usually better than two bowls on one mat.

## FAQ

### Do cats like to eat together?

Some tolerate it, but the AAFP-panel feeding paper says cats are solitary predators that prefer to eat often and alone. Bonded cats may eat near each other without trouble, but watch for one cat leaving early or waiting to eat last.

### How far apart should cat bowls be?

The sources I found do not give a distance. The RSPCA says resources should be in physically separated locations, with food out of sight of other cats' bowls. A different room, or a spot behind a visual barrier, is the goal. Bowls inches apart on one mat do not count as separate.

### How many food bowls do I need for two cats?

The RSPCA suggests one resource for each cat plus one extra, so at least three food bowls for two cats, in separate locations. If you cannot manage that, aim for at least one separate eating spot per cat.

### How can I tell if my cat is stressed eating near another cat?

Look for quiet signs: eating a little and leaving, waiting for the other cat to finish, bolting food, or staring at the other bowl. The 2018 feeding paper says cats may not show obvious signs of tension, so watch a few meals closely.

### Can I feed my cats in the same room if they don't fight?

You can, but not fighting does not always mean not stressed. If they must share a room, put the bowls as far apart as possible and block the line of sight.

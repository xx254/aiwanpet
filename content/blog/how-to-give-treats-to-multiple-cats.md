You rattle the treat bag once. Within three seconds the grey cat is vaulting the sofa, the tabby is pressed against your shin, and the small shy one is hovering at the edge of the rug, watching. You toss one treat to each. The grey cat inhales his, then lunges for the shy cat's. Now there is a hiss, a swat, and a treat that has skidded under the bookcase where nobody can reach it.

Giving treats to multiple cats is supposed to be the nice part of the day. In a multi-cat home it can turn into a small daily contest, and it quietly adds calories for the cats who are best at winning it. This post covers how to give treats to multiple cats fairly, how to keep count, and what to do when one cat should not be having them at all.

---

## Why Treat Time Goes Wrong in a Multi-Cat Home

A treat is a tiny, high-value meal. It disappears in one bite, which means there is no time for a slow or nervous cat to approach, and no way to "share" it. Whoever is closest and fastest gets it.

Think of it like throwing a handful of coins into a crowd. The people at the front catch most of them, and the ones at the back go home with nothing. Over a week of treat time, the same cats win and the same cats lose.

The AAFP feeding guidelines make the wider point that anxious cats, "in an attempt to avoid other pets in the household, may not access the food frequently enough and lose weight," and that feeding behaviours should be able to occur "even in a multi-pet home" ([AAFP, Feline Feeding Programs](https://www.catvets.com/guidelines/practice-guidelines/how-to-feed)). That statement is about meals, but treats follow the same logic. If a shy cat is edged out of the treats, it is probably being edged out of other things too.

## Know What Counts as a Treat

Most owners count the obvious ones: the crunchy bites, the squeezable tubes, the freeze-dried chicken. The World Small Animal Veterinary Association (WSAVA) defines treats more broadly. In its [guide to feeding treats to your cat](https://wsava.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/WSAVA_GuidetoTreats_Cats_251107.pdf), treats are "any food items that are given to your cat in addition to a complete and balanced diet," including "snacks, chews, supplements, and foods used to administer medication."

That last part matters in a multi-cat home. The dab of paste you use to hide a tablet, the lick of tuna you offer so a cat will go in the carrier, and the "just a nibble" of your own dinner all count. Each one is small, and together they can add up.

## How Many Treats Is Too Many?

The same WSAVA guide says treats "should always make up less than 10% of a cat's daily calorie intake," and that they should never replace a meal. It also gives a table of maximum daily treat calories by body weight. For a 2.5 kg cat the figure is 18 kcal a day, and for a 7 kg cat it is 28 kcal a day. The table assumes an average healthy adult cat in ideal body condition, and WSAVA suggests speaking to your vet team to work out your own cat's energy needs.

Two things follow from that:

- **The allowance is per cat, not per household.** If three cats share a pouch of treats, you need to know roughly what each one ate. A cat who gets two of every three treats may be well over their share.
- **A "treat" can be a large part of a small cat's day.** At 18 kcal, a small cat's whole treat budget may be only a few commercial treats. Check the label, or contact the manufacturer if no calorie count is printed, as WSAVA suggests.

## Ways to Give Treats Fairly

None of these is perfect. Pick the one that matches the cats you actually have.

### Treat one cat at a time

Shut the other cats out of the room, give a few treats, then swap. It is slow, but it removes competition entirely, and it lets you see exactly who has had what. It works best if you already have a mealtime routine where cats go to separate spaces. The [complete guide to feeding multiple cats separately](/blog/how-to-feed-multiple-cats-separately) has ideas for setting that up.

### Give treats at each cat's own station

If your cats already eat in separate spots, give the treat there, after the meal or in place of one part of it. The shy cat gets the same attention as the bold one, and nobody is lunging across a rug.

### Scatter instead of toss

Rather than tossing treats at three cats, drop a few in different corners of a room. The AAFP statement lists "forage feeding (putting food in different locations)" and puzzle feeders as parts of a feeding programme. For treats, scattering works best when each cat has an area to itself. In a crowded room a fast cat will simply sweep the lot.

### Use a treat puzzle, one per cat

A simple treat-dispensing toy slows a cat down and gives the treat some effort. If one cat is a thief, though, a puzzle can become a target: the thief just waits for the shy cat to roll it open. Our post on [puzzle feeders for multiple cats](/blog/puzzle-feeders-multiple-cats) goes through when this works and when it does not.

### Swap food treats for something else

WSAVA's guide suggests considering "alternative ways of spending fun, quality time with your cat." A two-minute play session with a wand toy, or a brush session, can stand in for a treat and gives a calorie-free reward. It also suits the cat who needs to lose weight.

## A Hypothetical Evening: Three Cats, One Bag of Treats

Picture a flat with three cats. Mango is fast and greedy. Olive is a middle-aged cat on a weight-loss plan from her vet. Pip is shy and slow, and tends to hang back.

A routine that could work: after dinner, Mango goes into the bedroom with the door closed and gets his treats from your hand, counted out. Olive gets a play session with a feather wand instead of a treat, and a few pieces of her own kibble kept back from her daily allowance as the "reward." Pip gets her treats in the hallway, where Mango can't reach, and you stay with her until she's finished.

Nobody is fighting, everyone has a turn, and you know who ate what. This is a made-up example, so adapt the details, but the pattern holds: one cat at a time or one cat per space, a counted amount, and a reward that suits each cat's health.

## When One Cat Should Not Have Treats

Some cats need to skip regular treats or have special ones. WSAVA notes that if a cat "has a medical condition, dietary sensitivities, or is being fed a veterinary-prescribed diet, conventional treats may not be appropriate," and says to ask your vet team if you are unsure.

In a multi-cat home, that can mean one cat sitting on the sidelines while the others get a snack. A few ways to handle it:

- **Give the restricted cat a vet-approved alternative.** Ask your vet whether a portion of their own food can serve as the treat.
- **Treat the others out of sight.** Give them treats in another room so the restricted cat is not watching.
- **Keep the bag out of reach.** A cat who is not allowed treats and is clever will find the bag. Store it somewhere cats cannot open.

## Skip the Dairy and the Table Scraps

Cat owners often reach for milk, cheese or a bit of leftover meat. WSAVA lists "milk and dairy products" among foods to avoid, noting that "many cats are lactose intolerant." It also says human food and table scraps "can be unhealthy and potentially dangerous to cats," and lists onions, garlic, grapes, raisins, chocolate and xylitol among toxic ingredients. In a multi-cat home, these risks multiply: dropped food on the kitchen floor is available to every cat, not just the one you meant to share with.

Treats that are made for cats, in a measured amount, are the safer option.

## When This Won't Work

Counting treats per cat only works if you can tell the cats apart at the moment of giving. If your cats are very food-driven, or one is a confirmed thief, the treat bag may simply be a source of conflict, and the kindest option may be no treats at all in the shared space. Separate rooms, closed doors and one-at-a-time sessions all need time and a free pair of hands, which is not realistic every evening. If one cat is guarding food or fighting over treats seriously, that is a behaviour matter worth raising with your vet, and our post on [cats that are aggressive about food in a multi-cat home](/blog/cat-aggressive-about-food-multi-cat-home) goes through what helps. None of the methods here can fix a medical problem, so if a cat is gaining or losing weight without explanation, see your vet.

## FAQ

### How many treats can I give each of my cats per day?

WSAVA says treats should make up less than 10% of a cat's daily calories, and its table lists 18 kcal a day for a 2.5 kg cat up to 28 kcal for a 7 kg cat. Those figures assume a healthy adult in ideal condition, so ask your vet what applies to each of your cats.

### How do I give treats to multiple cats without fighting?

Remove the competition. Treat one cat at a time behind a closed door, give treats at each cat's own feeding station, or use separate spaces. Avoid tossing treats to several cats at once, because the fastest cat will take most of them.

### Do medication treats count as treats?

Yes. WSAVA's definition of treats includes "foods used to administer medication." If you hide pills in a paste or pouch food, include it in the cat's treat calories. See [how to medicate a cat in a multi-cat home](/blog/medicating-cat-multi-cat-home) for ways to make sure the right cat gets the dose.

### Can I give my cat milk or cheese as a treat?

WSAVA recommends avoiding milk and dairy products because many cats are lactose intolerant. A cat-specific treat, or a small piece of the cat's own food, is a better choice.

### What if one cat is on a prescription diet and the others are not?

Ask your vet before giving that cat any treats. WSAVA says conventional treats may not be appropriate for cats on prescription diets, so the vet may suggest using a small portion of the prescription food instead.

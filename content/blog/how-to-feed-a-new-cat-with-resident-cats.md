The new cat has been home for two days. She is under the spare bed with a bowl of food you put down this morning, untouched. Outside the door, your resident cat is sitting very still, staring at the gap under it, and has also skipped breakfast. You now have two cats who are not eating, and a third who has noticed the spare room smells of fish and keeps trying to get in.

If you are working out how to feed a new cat when you already have cats, this is usually the week it gets hard. Food is the thing every cat in the house cares about most, which makes mealtimes either the best tool you have for the introduction or the place where it goes wrong.

Here is how to use meals to help the cats accept each other, what to do when somebody stops eating, and how to set up feeding once the doors are open for good.

---

## Why Mealtimes Matter So Much in a Cat Introduction

Cats are not natural dinner companions. The American Association of Feline Practitioners' consensus statement on [feline feeding programs](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11343346) notes that cats "prefer to eat often and alone," and that in multi-cat households cats "usually do not share their provided space equally." It also warns that competition for food can lead to conflict, and that cats may not show obvious signs of stress while it happens.

Now add a stranger. The [Toronto Humane Society's introduction guide](https://www.torontohumanesociety.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Introducing-Your-New-Cat-to-Your-Resident-Cat.pdf) points out that the average house cat spends its whole life without meeting another cat, and that a new arrival can feel very threatening to the cat who already lives there.

The good news is that food is also the most reliable way to change how the cats feel about each other. Shelter introduction plans are built around meals for exactly that reason: each cat learns that the smell, and later the sight, of the other cat comes just before something good.

Think of it like a new colleague who brings pastries to the office every morning. Nobody has decided whether they like them yet, but everyone has a reason to be glad when they walk in.

## Stage 1: Feed the New Cat in a Sanctuary Room

For the first days, the new cat lives in one room with everything they need. Toronto Humane's guide lists food, water, a litter box, a scratching post, comfortable resting places, hiding spots, places to climb and toys.

A few feeding details make this stage go better:

- **Keep the food away from the litter box.** The AAFP statement recommends that feeding stations sit away from litter boxes, and the [Cornell Feline Health Center](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/feeding-your-cat) notes that dirty food containers or nearby litter boxes can put a cat off eating. In a small room, use opposite corners.
- **Ask what the new cat was eating.** A shelter, foster or previous owner can tell you the brand, the format and how often. Starting on familiar food removes one change from a week full of changes. If you want to move them onto the household food, do it gradually and ask your vet how slowly to go.
- **Measure what goes down and what comes back.** A new cat who hides all day may only eat at night, so the empty bowl in the morning is the number that counts.

Meanwhile, the resident cats keep their normal meals in their normal places. Do not move their bowls to make room for the newcomer. Their routine is the one stable thing they have this week.

## Stage 2: Feed on Either Side of the Door

Once the new cat is eating in the sanctuary room, meals become part of the introduction. Toronto Humane describes the method clearly:

1. Feed both cats several small meals a day, a few feet away from either side of the closed door.
2. Before each meal, rub a sock around each cat's cheeks and chin, where many scent glands are, and place it beside the other cat's bowl.
3. Move the bowls a little closer to the door each day, *provided both cats eat without much hesitation and neither shows signs of fear*.
4. Take any uneaten food away after the meal. The guide is direct about it: "Do not free feed your cats."

That last point catches many households out. If the resident cats are used to a bowl of kibble that is always full, this is the time to switch to measured meals. A cat who can snack all day has little reason to care about the meal by the door. [Free Feeding vs Scheduled Feeding for Multiple Cats](/blog/free-feeding-vs-scheduled-feeding-multiple-cats) covers the change in more detail.

### If one cat will not eat near the door

Move that cat's bowl back to where they will eat, even if it is far down the hall, and start again from there. The distance only shrinks as fast as the more nervous cat allows. Rushing it teaches the opposite lesson: that the other cat's smell means a meal you cannot enjoy.

If you have more than one resident cat, give each their own bowl on the hallway side, spaced apart. The exercise does not help if two existing cats are squabbling over one plate while it happens.

## Stage 3: Meals With Sight but No Contact

When both cats eat calmly right next to the closed door, Toronto Humane's next step is to move the bowls back a few feet and start again with the cats able to see each other. A baby gate works, or a door opened a few inches and held with a door stop during the meal.

The same rule applies: bowls move closer only when both cats eat without much hesitation. If either cat shows fear or aggression, the guide suggests distracting with something they value, like treats, play or attention. After the meal, pick up the food and close the door.

This is also when you learn what kind of eaters you have. Watch for:

- A cat who bolts their food and then heads straight for the gate to see what the other cat has left.
- A cat who eats a few bites, looks up, and walks away.
- Prolonged staring, which Toronto Humane lists among the signs to interrupt.

The first is your likely food thief. The second is your likely victim. Make a note of both, because they shape how you set up feeding after the introduction is over.

## Stage 4: Feeding Once Everyone Shares the House

Toronto Humane's final stages are supervised time together, then gradual freedom. This is the point where many households go back to a single feeding spot because the cats seem fine with each other.

Resist that. The AAFP statement is specific: "Just because cats will eat together does not mean that they would not greatly benefit from separate feeding areas." It recommends multiple feeding stations that are visually separated.

In practice, that means:

- **One bowl per cat, in places where the cats cannot watch each other eat.** Around a corner, on a different counter, or in another room.
- **Fixed, measured meals** rather than one big bowl topped up all day, so you can see who is eating what.
- **A plan for the cats you flagged in Stage 3.** The fast eater will test every other bowl in the house. The hesitant eater may quietly give up their food rather than argue for it. [Why Is My Cat Stealing the Other Cat's Food](/blog/cat-stealing-other-cats-food) goes through the common patterns.

If the new cat arrives with a special diet, such as a prescription food or kitten food in a house of adults, separation is not optional. [Do You Need a Separate Room to Feed Cats Differently?](/blog/separate-room-feed-cats-differently) walks through room-based setups, and [How to Feed Cats at Different Life Stages in One Home](/blog/feeding-cats-different-life-stages) covers kittens and adults together.

## When Somebody Stops Eating

Some appetite loss is normal in the first day or so of a big change. Going several days without food is not.

The University of Illinois College of Veterinary Medicine explains that [if a cat goes without eating for a few days in a row](https://vetmed.illinois.edu/pet-health-columns/hepatic-lipidosis-when-cats-dont-eat/), there is a risk of hepatic lipidosis, a potentially fatal liver condition, and that overweight cats are at greater risk. Its advice is to seek veterinary attention promptly when a cat stops eating.

This applies to the resident cats as much as to the newcomer. A resident cat who is upset by the change may stop eating while all your attention is on the cat under the bed. Measuring every cat's meals during the introduction is how you catch it early. If any cat has eaten little or nothing for more than a day, call your vet rather than waiting for things to settle.

## When This Won't Work

The door-and-bowl method depends on having a door. In a studio flat or an open-plan home with no room that closes, a full sanctuary setup may not be possible, and you may need to improvise with a large crate, a screen or a borrowed room for the first week.

Some introductions also take far longer than owners expect, and the step-by-step guides deliberately give no fixed schedule: each stage lasts as long as the cats need. Some pairings never settle into easy shared meals, even with a careful start. If one cat is still hiding, still not eating, or still being chased away from food after several weeks, talk to your vet. They can check for a medical cause and, if needed, refer you to a behaviour professional.

No feeding setup can make two cats like each other. What it can do is take food off the list of things they fight about.

## FAQ

### Should I feed my new cat separately from my other cats?

Yes, at first in a separate sanctuary room, and then with bowls on either side of a closed door as part of the introduction. Even after the cats share the house, AAFP guidance recommends separate feeding stations that are out of sight of each other.

### How long should I feed the cats on either side of the door?

Until both cats eat calmly with their bowls right against the door. For some cats that takes days, for others weeks. Let the more nervous cat set the pace.

### Can I leave dry food out for all the cats during an introduction?

It is better not to. Toronto Humane's guide specifically says not to free feed during the process, and to take away uneaten food after each meal. Scheduled meals give the cats a reason to look forward to the time near the door and let you see who is eating.

### My new cat isn't eating. When should I worry?

A day of low appetite in a new home is common. If a cat has eaten little or nothing for more than a day, or seems lethargic or unwell, call your vet. Cats that go several days without food can develop serious liver problems.

### My resident cat keeps eating the new cat's food. What do I do?

Feed at set times, pick up bowls as soon as each cat finishes, and put the bowls where the fast eater cannot watch the slow one. If it keeps happening, the new cat needs a bowl that only they can reach.

Picture an evening a few weeks after Biscuit's operation. He is back to racing up the stairs, and he has a new hobby: finishing his own dinner, then strolling over to see what the others left. The kitten's dish is half full. The older cat has wandered off mid-meal. By the time you have rinsed the can, both bowls are clean and Biscuit is washing his face.

Feeding a neutered cat is much easier when you can see what goes into him. With several cats, that is the hard part. You can measure his portion carefully and still have no idea what he picked up elsewhere. This post covers what changes after neutering (including spaying), how to set a starting portion, and how to run meals when the other cats need something different.

---

## What Changes After Neutering

International Cat Care's guide to [neutering your cat](https://icatcare.org/articles/neutering-your-cat) says that once cats have been neutered, they are more prone to weight gain. It advises monitoring your cat and adjusting the diet if you are worried that weight is going on.

### Appetite can rise quickly

A [review of obesity risk in neutered cats](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11104111/) in the Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery (the author reports an honorarium from Nestlé Purina) says increased food intake is the most likely factor behind weight gain after neutering. It describes a study of male cats in which neutered cats started eating more almost immediately compared with intact ones. The difference was significant just three days after surgery.

A separate [study of nine adult male cats](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3935885/) concluded that early weight gain came from eating more, not from burning less energy. The cats ate significantly more once given free access to food, and their body weight and fat were significantly higher six months after neutering. It was a small study of males only.

### Males and females

The review says neutering is a risk factor for obesity, especially in males. It also reports one study in which the calories females needed to maintain their weight fell by 16% between four and 16 weeks after surgery, with no significant reduction in males over that period. The practical point is to watch every neutered cat, whatever the sex.

## Set a Starting Portion for Each Cat

The [Merck Veterinary Manual](https://www.merckvetmanual.com/management-and-nutrition/nutrition-small-animals/nutritional-requirements-of-small-animals) estimates daily calories from resting energy requirement (RER), which can be calculated as 70 × (body weight in kg)^0.75. For healthy adult cats, it then multiplies:

- **Intact:** 1.4 × RER
- **Neutered:** 1.2 × RER
- **Prone to obesity:** 1.0 × RER

For a 4 kg adult, that is roughly 277 kcal a day if intact and 238 kcal if neutered. These are estimates. Merck notes that an individual animal may need as much as 30% more or less than calculated. Do not use the adult figures for a growing kitten.

The review says holding back the extra food a cat wants may be enough for some, while others likely need less than before. Based on the intake increases reported in adult cats, it suggests that energy intake, if you know what it was, should be reduced by at least 11% and may need to be restricted by over 30%. Some cats have needed restriction of up to 50% to keep their earlier weight. That range is wide, so do not make a deep cut on your own. Ask your vet for a starting amount.

To turn calories into food, check the energy figure for your specific food. Our guide to [how much to feed each cat](/blog/how-much-to-feed-each-cat-multi-cat-home) walks through the math. Then [weigh the portion on a scale](/blog/weigh-cat-food-scale-vs-scoop-multi-cat-home). International Cat Care's [feeding guide](https://icatcare.org/articles/feeding-your-cat-or-kitten) notes that dry food is very energy-dense, so the correct amount may look small. Count [treats](/blog/how-to-give-treats-to-multiple-cats) as part of the day.

### Do you need a special food?

The review reports more weight and fat gain after neutering with energy-dense diets, but also says the effect of diet type on obesity risk is unclear because findings vary. Ask your vet whether your cat's current food still suits them. A food sold for neutered cats still has to be portioned.

## If You Are Neutering a Kitten

Kittens are still growing. International Cat Care says they have higher nutritional needs than adult cats and should be fed food designed for their life stage. Do not put a kitten on an adult cat's reduced ration. Ask your vet when to change food and how much to offer.

In a [small study of female kittens](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5468748/), run and funded by a pet food company's research centre (Mars), 13 were included in the analysis. All were individually fed to keep an ideal body condition. Those neutered at 31 weeks showed a rapid increase in food intake and body weight afterwards. Those neutered at 19 weeks did not show that sudden jump. This does not show that early neutering prevents obesity. International Cat Care advises that neutering should routinely take place at around four months of age, so talk timing through with your vet.

If your kitten shares a home with adults, see [how to feed kitten and adult food side by side](/blog/kitten-food-vs-adult-cat-food-multi-cat-home).

## Running Meals for a Neutered Cat Among Other Cats

Think of his daily ration as a budget. Every unwatched bowl is a second wallet he can spend from, and your careful measuring only tracks the first.

### Move from open bowls to portions

The review concludes that the evidence supports avoiding free-choice feeding in neutered cats. It also notes that studies have not identified a link between feeding practices and how common obesity is, so meals are no guarantee. Their practical value in a group is that you decide how much goes down, and for whom. Our comparison of [free feeding and scheduled feeding](/blog/free-feeding-vs-scheduled-feeding-multiple-cats) can help you choose a routine.

Meals do not have to mean two large ones. International Cat Care advises, where possible, dividing a cat's daily ration into at least five portions. Split the daily amount; do not add to it.

### Give each cat a protected spot

Choose the setup that fits your day:

1. **Separate rooms.** Feed with doors closed, then pick up every bowl before opening them. It needs no new equipment, but someone has to be there.
2. **Supervised stations.** Space the bowls well apart, stay nearby, and lift each one when its owner walks away.
3. **Timed feeders.** These help with meal timing. A timer alone does not control which cat eats the food.
4. **Selective feeders.** These use a microchip or tag to limit a bowl to a particular cat. Consider one if a cat needs to eat while you are out.

If raiding is the main trouble, read [why cats steal each other's food](/blog/cat-stealing-other-cats-food) before changing equipment.

### Decide which bowl needs guarding

This is easy to get backwards. If your neutered cat steals from a slow eater, restricting the slow eater's bowl is what protects it. A selective lid on the neutered cat's own bowl stops others taking his portion, but does nothing to stop him visiting theirs. Every other bowl he can reach must be restricted to its owner or picked up.

## Check the Cat, Not Just the Bowl

An empty dish does not tell you whether your plan is working. International Cat Care says regularly weighing your cats, with cat scales or baby scales, can help you keep a close eye on their health. Write down the date and number, and ask your vet how often to weigh after surgery.

Check body condition too. Cornell's page on [feline obesity](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/obesity) describes a nine-point scale on which 4.5 to 5 is ideal and a score above 6.5 or 7 counts as overweight. Our [body condition guide for multi-cat homes](/blog/cat-body-condition-score-multi-cat-home) explains how to check each cat.

If a cat is already overweight, do not crash-diet them. Cornell's page says weight reduction should be directed by a veterinarian and warns that a sudden starvation diet puts a cat at risk of serious problems such as the liver disease hepatic lipidosis. It suggests gradual loss, perhaps one or two percent of body weight a week. If your cat stops eating, call your vet promptly. For longer-term planning, see [helping an overweight cat in a multi-cat home](/blog/overweight-cat-diet-multiple-cats).

## A Hypothetical Three-Cat Plan

Picture a home with Biscuit, recently neutered; Wren, a kitten; and Olive, an older cat who eats in stages.

Their owner asks the vet how much each cat needs. Open bowls come up. Each cat's daily food is weighed separately and divided into meals. Biscuit eats in the hallway, Wren in the bathroom with the door closed, and Olive on a surface she can comfortably reach and Biscuit cannot. When nobody can supervise, unfinished food is picked up, and Olive gets the rest of her share later.

If Biscuit's weight climbs, the owner looks for leaks, such as a missed bowl, a second breakfast from someone else in the house or extra treats. Then they take their notes to the vet.

## When This Won't Work

- **Nobody can serve the extra meals.** Several portions a day may not fit your schedule. Build a routine you can keep.
- **Food comes from somewhere else.** Meals at home cannot control what a cat gets from neighbours, hunting or an open bin.
- **The household is not coordinated.** If two people each serve a full breakfast, one careful portion becomes two. Use a shared checklist.
- **Weight or appetite changes despite a tight routine.** Unexplained changes need a vet check rather than more adjustments.
- **A device makes a cat nervous.** Introduce a moving lid gradually using [these steps for a cat afraid of a feeder lid](/blog/cat-afraid-of-feeder-lid), and make sure the cat keeps eating. A lid also does not set the portion for you.

## FAQ

### Should I feed my cat less after neutering?

Many cats need their intake managed, but the right amount varies. Merck's calorie estimate is lower for a neutered adult than for an intact one. Ask your vet for a starting amount, especially for a kitten, then adjust with them based on weight and body condition.

### Why is my cat so hungry after being neutered?

Increased food intake after neutering is documented in the research above. Hunger can have other causes too. If it is intense or unusual, or comes with weight loss or other changes, contact your vet.

### Can my neutered cat eat the same food as my other cats?

Possibly, if your vet agrees it suits each cat. Cats can share a food and still need different amounts, so serve and track portions separately. Kittens and cats on prescribed diets need their own advice.

### Can I leave dry food out for a neutered cat?

The review supports avoiding free-choice feeding in neutered cats. If another cat needs to graze, keep that food where your neutered cat cannot get it, or offer it in supervised portions.

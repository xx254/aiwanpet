Most multi-cat feeding advice assumes everyone is trying to eat less. This one is the opposite problem, and it is harder.

A cat who needs to gain weight needs calorie-dense food available often, ideally free access. A cat who needs to lose weight needs measured portions and no free access. Put them in the same house and the two plans actively sabotage each other — the overweight cat finds the rich food first, and the thin cat, who is often the more timid one, gets what is left.

Here is how to run both at once.

---

## First: Why Is the Cat Underweight?

This matters more than any feeding tactic, because weight loss in a cat is a symptom before it is a feeding problem.

**If your cat is losing weight while eating normally or eating more than usual**, that is a medical finding and it needs a vet. The common causes — hyperthyroidism, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, intestinal disease, and cancer among them — are diagnosed with bloodwork, not guessed at. Feeding more calories to a cat with untreated hyperthyroidism does not fix the underlying condition.

**If your cat is eating less than they should**, work out why: dental pain, nausea, competition at the bowl, stress, or a food they simply do not like.

**And if the cat is simply being out-competed**, that is the case this article is about. It is common, it is under-recognised, and it usually coexists with the first two.

Get the vet visit done first. Everything below assumes an underlying cause is being addressed.

---

## The Core Conflict

| | Underweight cat needs | Overweight cat needs |
|---|---|---|
| **Access** | Frequent, ideally free | Measured, restricted |
| **Food type** | Calorie-dense, high protein, palatable | Lower calorie, satiety-oriented |
| **Meal pattern** | Small meals through the day | Structured, defined portions |
| **Leftovers** | Left down so they can return | Removed immediately |

Every row is a direct contradiction. This is why "just feed them separately at mealtimes" does not work here — the underweight cat's requirement is not a mealtime requirement. They need to be able to come back at 2pm and again at 10pm, and any system that depends on you being present fails at exactly those moments.

## The Approaches That Work

### 1. Protected Free Access for the Thin Cat

This is the cleanest solution to the actual problem, because it is the only one that gives the underweight cat unsupervised access without giving it to anybody else.

The underweight cat's calorie-dense food goes in a bowl only they can open. It stays available all day. They eat five small meals if that is what they want. The overweight cat can sit next to it indefinitely and get nothing.

Meanwhile the overweight cat gets their measured portions on schedule, from their own bowl.

This is precisely the case selective feeding was built for, and it is worth noting that the direction is unusual: most people buy one to stop a cat eating, and here you are buying one to let a cat eat.

The [Aiwan Cat Food Shield](https://aiwanpet.com) covers the bowl your thin cat already eats from and opens only for their microchip or tag. Keeping the familiar bowl matters more than usual here — an underweight cat is often a fussy or anxious eater, and changing their bowl at the same time as everything else is a good way to reduce their intake further.

### 2. Vertical Separation

Underweight cats are frequently seniors or the more timid cat in the household, and overweight cats are frequently the confident one. Height exploits that difference.

Put the thin cat's food somewhere the overweight cat finds inconvenient to reach — a stable shelf, the top of a cat tree, a counter they are permitted on. For an arthritic senior this needs steps or a ramp rather than a jump, and it needs checking that they can actually get up there comfortably every time.

This is free and it works in many homes. It is not reliable — a motivated overweight cat will make the climb — but it reduces casual theft.

### 3. A Room With a Cat-Sized Door

If the size difference is substantial, a door wedged open a few inches, or a microchip cat flap into a spare room, can create space only the smaller cat uses. Practical in some homes, impossible in others.

### 4. Supervised Top-Up Meals

The thin cat gets two or three additional feeding sessions a day with you present. Genuinely useful — and genuinely dependent on you being home. Best used alongside one of the approaches above rather than as the whole plan.

## Getting Calories Into a Cat Who Won't Eat Much

Protecting the food is only half of it. The other half is making it worth eating.

**Warm it.** Wet food warmed to roughly body temperature releases far more aroma, and appetite in cats is driven heavily by smell. This is the highest-yield trick in the list, especially for senior cats whose sense of smell has declined.

**Increase the calorie density rather than the volume.** A cat with a small appetite cannot solve a calorie deficit by eating more of a low-calorie food. Kitten food and recovery diets pack more calories into the same mouthfuls — ask your vet which is appropriate, particularly if there is kidney disease in the picture, where protein and phosphorus need to be considered.

**Offer small amounts often.** A large bowl can be off-putting. Small, fresh servings several times a day usually beat one big plate.

**Fresh, not sitting out.** Wet food that has been down for hours is unappetising and, past a couple of hours at room temperature, not safe. A cover keeps dust and other cats out; it does not refrigerate. See [Can You Feed Wet Food to Multiple Cats?](/blog/feed-wet-food-multiple-cats).

**Check their mouth.** Dental pain is one of the most common reasons a cat quietly reduces intake, and it is easy to miss.

**Reduce mealtime stress.** A cat who eats in a quiet place, out of sight of housemates, with an escape route, eats more. See [Cat Aggressive About Food](/blog/cat-aggressive-about-food-multi-cat-home).

## Keeping the Dieting Cat Honest at the Same Time

The risk in all of this is that the thin cat's rich food becomes a free buffet for the cat who is meant to be losing weight. A single day of that undoes a week of careful portioning, and because it is invisible, the conclusion drawn is usually that the diet is not working.

**Weigh both cats every two weeks** and write it down. Do not rely on how they look — gradual change is invisible day to day, and in a long-coated cat it is invisible for months.

**Trust the scale over the plan.** If the thin cat is not gaining and the heavy cat is not losing, the most likely explanation is not that both plans are wrong. It is that the food is going to the wrong cat.

Details on the weight-loss side are in [Helping an Overweight Cat Lose Weight in a Multi-Cat Home](/blog/overweight-cat-diet-multiple-cats) and [Portion Control for Cats in a Multi-Cat Household](/blog/cat-portion-control-multiple-cats).

## Where This Gets Hard

Two cats at opposite ends of the weight spectrum in a small home with one feeding area is a genuinely difficult setup, and it may not be solvable by placement alone.

A senior underweight cat with arthritis cannot use vertical separation, which removes the cheapest option exactly when you need it most.

And a cat losing weight for an untreated medical reason will keep losing it no matter how good your feeding logistics are. If the numbers are not moving after a month of a well-executed plan, the answer is another vet visit, not another feeding strategy.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much weight loss in a cat is a problem?**
Cats are small, so the percentages move fast. A cat dropping from 5kg to 4.5kg has lost 10% of their body weight — in human terms, substantial. Any unexplained loss is worth a vet visit, and gradual loss in a senior cat should never be written off as normal ageing.

**Can I just leave kitten food out for the thin cat during the day?**
Only if the other cat genuinely cannot reach it. Free-fed calorie-dense food in an accessible bowl is the fastest way to add weight to the cat who is meant to be losing it.

**My thin cat eats constantly and is still losing weight. What's going on?**
Increased appetite with weight loss is a classic presentation of hyperthyroidism and of diabetes. That combination warrants bloodwork promptly rather than a change in feeding.

**Should I free-feed everyone and accept the overweight cat will stay heavy?**
It is a real trade-off, and for some households with a seriously ill thin cat it is the right short-term call. It is not a good long-term position — obesity carries meaningful risks of its own — which is why protected access is usually the better answer.

Picture a kitchen floor with three bowls in a neat row beside the fridge. It looks fair. It looks organised. Then dinner starts: the ginger cat eats from his own bowl and glances sideways at the next one, the tortoiseshell eats with her shoulders hunched, and the grey cat sits in the doorway and waits. You bought three bowls, but you have really built one feeding station with three plates on it.

If you have been wondering how many feeding stations you need for multiple cats, the short version is more than most of us start with, and spread further apart than feels natural. This post covers a practical rule of thumb, what the vet sources actually say, where to put the stations in a normal home, and what to do when you have no spare room.

---

## So, How Many Feeding Stations Do You Need?

### The "one per cat, plus a spare" rule of thumb

There is no single official number. What the guidance does agree on is the direction: more spots, further apart, with each cat having a place of their own.

The AAFP and ISFM environmental needs guidelines, as summarised by [VCA Hospitals](https://vcahospitals.com/pediatric/kitten/health-wellness/feline-environmental-needs-guidelines), say resources "should be available in multiple places, especially if you have a multi-cat household." They add that "cats should have at least two options for each resource, such as two feeding areas and two resting areas," and that "in multi-cat households, each cat should have access to their own resources."

PetMD's article on [feeding cats separately](https://www.petmd.com/cat/nutrition/feeding-multiple-cats), written by a veterinarian, puts it simply: "Every cat should have their own spot to eat and drink."

So the working rule is:

- **Two cats:** at least two stations, in separate places. Three is better if one cat is shy or on a different food.
- **Three or more cats:** one station per cat as the target. If you cannot manage that, aim for as many as your layout allows and use timing to cover the gap.
- **One cat with special needs** (a prescription diet, a medication, a fussy senior): treat their station as a separate project, even if the others share.

This is a rule of thumb built from the sources above, not a measured number. Your cats may need more or fewer.

### What counts as a separate station

This is where most homes go wrong. A station is separate when the cat eating at it does not feel watched or crowded by another cat. Bowls a hand's width apart do not count. A [Psychology Today piece on cats eating in peace](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/fellow-creatures/202603/can-your-cat-eat-their-meals-in-peace) makes the same point: bowls placed close together "kind of turns them into one food bowl."

A useful test: stand where the shy cat would sit while eating. Can she see another cat's head? Can another cat walk past within a step or two? If yes, you have one station, not two.

## Why One Row of Bowls Fails

Think of a restaurant with a single long table and no walls. The confident diner eats first and sits where he likes. The nervous one pushes food around her plate or leaves. Nobody has to be rude. The setup does the work.

Cats are the same. They do not need to fight to win a bowl. Standing in the doorway is enough. PetMD describes cats that "bully their way into everybody else's food bowls" and dominant cats "harassing their more timid housemates at mealtime." If that sounds like your home, [My Cat Gets Chased Away From the Food Bowl](/blog/cat-chased-away-from-food-bowl-multi-cat-home) covers the quiet side of it, and [Do Cats Prefer to Eat Alone?](/blog/do-cats-prefer-to-eat-alone-multi-cat-home) explains why.

The Cornell Feline Health Center also notes in its [feeding guide](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/feeding-your-cat) that "cats can be choosy about where they eat," and that "the presence of other animals, dirty food containers, or nearby litter boxes can deter a cat from eating." Location matters as much as number.

## Where to Put the Stations

### Think in zones, not corners

Walk through your home and mark the places a cat could plausibly eat: the kitchen, a bedroom, a bathroom or utility room, a hallway nook out of the main traffic route, a shelf or cat tree platform.

PetMD suggests setting up stations "in bathrooms, bedrooms, or any low-stress spot," keeping them "as far apart as is practical," and feeding a climber "on a stable shelf or on top of a sturdy cat tree." It also calls feeding each cat in a separate room behind a closed door "the ultimate solution for serious mealtime problems."

### Keep food away from the litter box

VCA's summary of the guidelines says each resource should be "in its own location separate from the others," with the food bowl not near the litter box. Cornell's point about litter boxes agrees. If your only free corner is next to the tray, it is not a feeding station.

### Break line of sight

Even two stations in one room can work if something blocks the view: a cupboard end, a sofa back, a door. The key question is whether the cat can see the other one eating. If not, her shoulders usually drop.

## A Hypothetical Three-Cat Layout

Picture a two-bedroom flat with three cats: Biscuit (confident, fast), Juniper (shy), and Otis (older, slower). All three are fed in the hallway, side by side.

A workable rearrangement might be Biscuit in the kitchen with the door closed, Juniper on a shelf in the bedroom out of view of the doorway, and Otis in the bathroom on a non-slip mat with time to finish. Meals begin together, and the doors open when the last cat has finished and bowls are lifted. This is an invented household and every home differs, but it shows the idea: three cats, three places, no audience.

## When You Cannot Get One Station Per Cat

Most homes cannot give every cat a private room. These are the usual fixes, in rough order of effort.

### Stagger the meals

Feed cat one, let her finish, lift the bowl, then feed cat two in the same spot. One station can serve several cats if the meals do not overlap. This only works with measured meals rather than food left down all day. See [free feeding vs scheduled feeding](/blog/free-feeding-vs-scheduled-feeding-multiple-cats) for the trade-offs. PetMD's key takeaways note that free feeding "often leads to overeating and food stealing."

### Use height and doors you already have

A cat tree platform or sturdy shelf adds a station without using floor space. It suits agile adult cats, not stiff seniors, kittens or cats with mobility issues. A bathroom or bedroom door that you can shut for ten to fifteen minutes costs nothing.

### Protect the one bowl that matters most

Some homes add a selective feeder at the station where a prescription diet or a diet-restricted cat eats. That protects one bowl rather than needing a room for every cat. We compare this with the other options in [Do Microchip Cat Feeders Actually Work?](/blog/do-microchip-cat-feeders-work).

## How to Check Whether Your Stations Are Working

A new layout is a guess until you see how the cats respond. For a week or two, watch for these:

- **Does the shy cat eat at normal times?** Eating at 3am after everyone is asleep means she still does not feel safe.
- **Does anyone hover over a bowl that is not theirs?** Then the stations are still too close.
- **Is anyone gaining or losing weight?** A [body condition check](/blog/cat-body-condition-score-multi-cat-home) every few weeks is more reliable than guessing.

If one cat stops eating, do not assume it is social. A vet check comes before any rearranging, and [My Cat Isn't Eating: How to Tell Which Cat Skipped a Meal](/blog/cat-not-eating-multi-cat-home) will help you work out who it is.

## When This Won't Work

- **Some cats guard doors and corridors.** Moving bowls apart will not help if one cat patrols the whole flat. You will need doors closed, or the cats fed at different times.
- **Small homes limit what is possible.** With one room and no shelves, timing and a protected bowl may be your only options.
- **More stations means more work.** More bowls in more places means more washing and more tracking of who ate what.
- **It will not fix tension that lives elsewhere.** If the cats fight over beds, litter boxes or windows, food is only part of it.
- **A sudden change in appetite is medical until proven otherwise.** More stations will not fix a sore tooth or an illness.
- **A closed-door routine needs someone home.** If you are out for hours at a time, you will need another way to protect each bowl.

## FAQ

### How many feeding stations do I need for two cats?

At least two, in separate places, and ideally out of sight of each other. VCA's summary of the AAFP and ISFM guidelines recommends at least two options for each resource, and PetMD says every cat should have their own spot to eat and drink.

### Is a row of bowls in the same room enough?

Usually not. Bowls placed close together can act as one shared bowl, and a confident cat can hold the whole row without any fighting. Put the stations in different rooms, or at least behind something that blocks the view.

### Can two cats share one feeding station?

Sometimes. If both cats are relaxed, eat at the same pace and neither is on a special diet, a shared station can work. If one cat waits for the other to leave, eats very fast, or loses weight while the other gains it, split them up.

### Where should I not put a cat's food?

Not next to the litter box, and not in a busy corridor where the cat is walked past while eating. Cornell notes that nearby litter boxes and the presence of other animals can put a cat off eating.

### What if I can't give each cat a separate room?

Stagger the meals so only one cat eats at a time, use height with a shelf or cat tree, or use any door you can close for the length of the meal. Our post on [whether you need a separate room to feed cats differently](/blog/separate-room-feed-cats-differently) goes through the alternatives.

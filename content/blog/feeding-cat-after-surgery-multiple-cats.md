You bring her home in the carrier around five o'clock, still a little wobbly, with a plastic cone around her head and a shaved patch on her belly. You set a small bowl of food down in the kitchen. She takes two half-hearted licks, bumps the rim of her cone against the bowl and backs away. Before you've hung up your coat, the other two cats have arrived and the bowl is empty. Now you have no idea whether she ate anything at all.

Feeding a cat after surgery is fairly simple when it's the only cat in the house. You offer a small meal, watch, and wait for the appetite to come back. In a multi-cat home the recovering cat eats slowly, or not at all, and someone else is always ready to finish the bowl. On the one day you really need to know how much she ate, the empty bowl tells you nothing.

This guide covers what vets say about eating after surgery, the warning signs to watch for, and how to set up the first few days so she gets her food and you can see whether she ate it.

## What to Expect From Your Cat's Appetite After Surgery

### A slow first day is normal

The American College of Veterinary Surgeons' guide to [postoperative care](https://www.acvs.org/small-animal/postoperative-care/) says: "Some pets may eat less or skip meals during the first 24 hours after surgery." It adds that "appetite typically improves as the effects of anesthesia resolve and pain is well controlled."

So a cat who picks at her dinner the evening she comes home isn't necessarily in trouble. What matters is the trend over the next day or two. In a multi-cat home you can only see that trend if you know what she actually ate.

### Follow the discharge sheet first

Your vet's instructions come before any general advice, including this post. If the sheet says when to offer food, how much and what kind, do that. If it doesn't, ask before you leave the clinic. It's the same habit that helps the night before, which we cover in our guide to [fasting a cat before surgery when you have other cats](/blog/fasting-cat-before-surgery-multiple-cats).

### Don't switch foods, and keep water down

ACVS advises that "sudden diet changes should be avoided unless specifically directed by the veterinary team." Her usual food in a smaller portion is the safe default. If your vet does send her home on a recovery or prescription diet, our guide to [feeding prescription food in a multi-cat home](/blog/prescription-cat-food-multiple-cats) covers how to keep it hers.

ACVS also says "access to fresh water should always be available unless otherwise instructed," and that "decreased water intake or signs of dehydration should be reported." Put water wherever she's recovering, not just where the household bowl usually sits.

## Feeding After Specific Procedures

### After dental extractions

PetMD's [cat tooth extraction recovery guide](https://www.petmd.com/cat/general-health/cat-tooth-extraction-recovery-guide), written by a vet, says: "Offer soft foods for several days after your cat's tooth extraction surgery. These include moist food, semi-moist food, and even kibble that is soaked in water."

So for a while her food will look and smell different from everyone else's, and soft, smelly food draws a crowd. PetMD also lists refusing food and drooling among the signs of a mouth infection worth calling the vet about. You can only notice that she's refusing food if nobody else is eating it for her.

### When she's wearing a cone

VCA Animal Hospitals' guide to [Elizabethan collars in cats](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/elizabethan-collars-in-cats) says: "Your cat can eat and drink normally with a properly fitted E-collar," and that "most cats quickly adapt." It also says you "may need to elevate or change the shape of the food and water bowls," and that "some cats find it easier to eat from a plate or saucer rather than a bowl, while others will initially refuse to eat while wearing the E-collar."

Cat Protection Society of NSW's [factsheet on Elizabethan collars](https://catprotection.org.au/cat-care-factsheets/factsheet-elizabethan-collars/) is more cautious: "Some cats are totally unable to eat or drink unassisted when wearing an E-collar." It recommends a bowl that is "high off the ground and with a smaller diameter than the rim of the collar."

Think of it as trying to eat with a lampshade on: the rim hits the bowl before her mouth gets there. Try a raised dish or a saucer. If she still can't manage, ask your vet whether the cone can come off at mealtimes while you watch. Don't remove it without asking. ACVS notes that "licking or chewing at an incision can delay healing and increase the risk of infection."

## When Not Eating Becomes Urgent

VCA's article on [fatty liver syndrome in cats](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/liver-disease-fatty-liver-syndrome-in-cats) says hepatic lipidosis usually follows "a period of anorexia (little or no eating) for three to four consecutive days," and that it is more likely "if the cat was overweight or obese before the anorexia began." It warns that "the disease is fatal if not treated rapidly and aggressively."

One skipped dinner isn't a crisis. But a cat who has been quietly not eating for two or three days while the bowl keeps emptying is exactly the cat who slips through in a busy house. ACVS lists "poor appetite that persists beyond the expected recovery period, vomiting, or concerns about hydration" as reasons to contact your vet, along with "worsening lethargy, weakness, or behavior changes that do not improve over time."

If you honestly don't know whether she's eating, that's a reason to call. Our post on [how to tell which cat skipped a meal](/blog/cat-not-eating-multi-cat-home) covers simple ways to find out.

## Why Recovery Is Harder in a Multi-Cat Home

**She's the slowest eater in the house.** Groggy, sore and maybe in a cone, she takes a few bites and walks off to lie down. An unattended bowl goes to whoever gets there first.

**Her food is the most interesting food in the house.** Softened food after dental work or a new recovery diet appeals to the healthy cats too.

**The other cats may not welcome her back.** A veterinarian writing for Embrace Pet Insurance on [non-recognition aggression](https://www.embracepetinsurance.com/waterbowl/article/feline-non-recognition-aggression) explains that "veterinary clinics have a distinct odor," that medications can "give your cat a different scent," and that stressed cats "release copious amounts of stress hormones." The housemate who usually shares the sofa may hiss at her instead. The article says most cases "will subside within a few days to weeks." Until then, a shared feeding spot can turn into a standoff.

**Her medication may be in her food.** If pain relief or antibiotics go into a meal, every bite another cat steals is a missed dose for her and a dose the thief was never meant to have. Our guide to [medicating a cat in a multi-cat home](/blog/medicating-cat-multi-cat-home) goes into this in detail.

## A Simple Setup for the First Few Days

### 1. Give her a recovery room

A spare room, bathroom or quiet bedroom works. Put in her bed, a litter tray, water, and a dish she can reach with her cone on. ACVS says running, jumping and stairs "should be limited as directed by your veterinary team," and a small room without high shelves helps with that too.

A closed door also deals with the scent problem. The Embrace article advises keeping the cats apart until the aggression subsides, then reintroducing them gradually. For making a room setup work day to day, see whether [you need a separate room to feed cats differently](/blog/separate-room-feed-cats-differently).

### 2. Offer small meals and write down what she eats

Put a measured amount down, wait, and see what's left. With the door shut, what's gone is what she ate. A note on your phone with the time and roughly how much she ate turns "I think she ate something" into a trend you can read out to the vet.

### 3. Feed everyone else on schedule, somewhere else

Keep the other cats' routine as close to normal as you can, in their usual spot at their usual times, so nobody has a reason to go looking for her plate. If someone is especially determined, our guide to [why cats steal each other's food](/blog/cat-stealing-other-cats-food) explains the pattern.

### 4. Bring her back slowly

Once the hissing has stopped and the vet has cleared her for normal activity, let her back into the house a little at a time. Keep feeding her separately until you're confident she's eating well and the others leave her plate alone.

## Picture a Typical Week

Picture a three-cat house. Olive has had three teeth out. Rufus and Tansy are healthy and eat everything.

**Day one:** Olive goes straight into the spare room with soft food on a saucer and fresh water. She eats a little and sleeps. Rufus hisses at the gap under the door.

**Days two and three:** Her appetite picks up, and you note what she eats at each meal. Rufus and Tansy eat in the kitchen, on time, as usual.

**Days four to seven:** The hissing stops and Olive wants out. But she's still on soft food and still slow, and the first time everyone eats in the kitchen, Tansy finishes Olive's saucer before Olive has finished sniffing it.

The recovery room handles the first few days. Feeding her separately once she's back among the others takes longer. Our guide on [how to feed multiple cats separately](/blog/how-to-feed-multiple-cats-separately) covers that part.

## When This Won't Work

None of this replaces your vet. If she isn't eating, is vomiting, seems more lethargic rather than less, or the incision looks wrong, call the clinic. A feeding setup can't fix pain or infection.

A recovery room isn't possible in every home. In a studio flat, or with a cat who panics when shut in, you may need to sit with her while she eats and pick up the plate when she's done. And a cat who won't eat in a cone, whatever you do with the bowl, is a question for the vet, not something to wait out.

## FAQ

### How long after surgery can my cat eat?

Follow your vet's discharge instructions, because timing depends on the procedure. ACVS says some pets eat less or skip meals in the first 24 hours, and that appetite usually improves as the anaesthetic wears off and pain is controlled.

### Is it normal for my cat not to eat after surgery?

A small appetite on the first day is common. Call your vet if it doesn't improve. VCA says fatty liver disease usually follows three to four days of little or no eating, and is more likely in cats who were overweight beforehand.

### Can a cat eat with a cone on?

Usually. VCA says most cats can eat and drink normally with a properly fitted E-collar, though a raised bowl or a flat plate may help. Some cats can't manage at all. Ask your vet before removing the cone for meals.

### Why is my other cat hissing at my cat after surgery?

Most likely it's the smell. A veterinarian writing for Embrace Pet Insurance explains that clinic odours, medication and stress hormones can change how the returning cat smells. Keep them apart for now and reintroduce gradually.

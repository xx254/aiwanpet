With two cats, selective feeding is close to trivial. One cat needs their food protected, the other doesn't, and you are done.

Three is a different problem. There are more relationships to manage, someone is usually waiting for someone else to finish, and the layout of the room starts to matter as much as the hardware. Most of the questions we get about multi-cat setups are really questions about three or more.

Here is how to plan it.

---

## Step 1: Work Out Who Actually Needs Protecting

Not every cat needs a protected bowl. Before you buy anything, sort your three into categories.

**Cats whose food must be protected from others.** The prescription diet, the measured weight-loss portion, the hydrolysed protein during a trial.

**Cats who must be prevented from eating something.** A cat with a food allergy needs protection from *other* bowls, which is a different problem from protecting their own.

**Cats with no dietary constraint at all.** They eat normal food and maintain a normal weight.

The distinction between the first two categories decides your layout. Protecting one cat's bowl is straightforward. Preventing one cat from reaching two other bowls means every other bowl needs a cover — which is a materially bigger setup, and worth knowing before you order.

A common three-cat pattern:

> **Cat A** — kidney disease, renal diet, needs a protected bowl
> **Cat B** — overweight, measured portions, needs a protected bowl
> **Cat C** — healthy, eats normally, bowl needs no cover

That is two protected bowls, not three.

---

## Step 2: Placement — The Part That Decides Whether It Works

Hardware failures in multi-cat homes are rare. Placement failures are constant.

**Separate the bowls properly.** Cats are not a family sitting down to dinner; free-living cats eat alone. Bowls lined up side by side create competition that has nothing to do with the food. Put real distance between them — different sides of a room at minimum, different rooms if you can. Several feet apart is not the same as across the room, and across the room is much better.

**Break the sight lines.** Distance matters less than visibility. A bowl behind a sofa, around a corner, or on the far side of a doorway lets a cat eat without monitoring a housemate. Two bowls ten feet apart in an open room can be worse than two bowls six feet apart with furniture between them.

**Give every bowl an escape route.** Never place a feeder in a dead end, an alcove, or a corner where a cat eating has their back to the only exit. This single mistake produces more "my cat won't use the feeder" cases than any mechanical issue.

**Use height.** Cats use vertical space to avoid each other. A confident cat's bowl on the floor and a nervous cat's bowl on a stable shelf or cat tree often solves a conflict that no amount of horizontal distance would.

**Keep RFID fields apart.** Two selective feeders placed close together can interfere with each other's reads. If you are running two protected bowls, distance helps the electronics as well as the peace.

**Avoid metal.** Metal shelving directly under or behind a feeder degrades RFID reads. If one bowl is unreliable and another is fine, look at what it is sitting on before blaming the chip.

## Step 3: Decide Between Scheduled and Free-Fed

With three cats, this decision drives everything else.

**Scheduled meals** give you a known number of calories per cat and let you observe every meal. The cost is that everybody eats at the same time, which concentrates competition into two windows a day. It also requires you to be there, unless an automatic feeder is handling the timing.

**Free feeding with protected bowls** spreads consumption across the day, which suits cats better and largely removes competition — each cat eats when they like, at a bowl only they can open. The cost is that you lose the daily visual check, and portion control on a free-fed protected bowl requires you to measure the day's ration into it rather than topping it up.

**The hybrid most three-cat households land on:** wet food scheduled and supervised, dry food free-fed in protected bowls. You get eyes on everyone twice a day and the rest of the day runs itself.

## Step 4: Handle the Lurker

Every three-cat home has one. Cat C sits three feet from Cat A's protected bowl and waits. They are not being aggressive. They are being patient.

Lurking works, because the protection is on the bowl, not on the room. When the authorised cat steps away with the lid still open, the lurker takes their opportunity. If your feeder closes when the cat leaves, that window is short — but a fast cat needs very little time.

**What actually helps:**

- **Move the protected bowl out of sight of the common area.** A lurker who has to commit to a separate room usually loses interest.
- **Feed the lurker first.** A cat with a full stomach lurks much less. If Cat C eats five minutes before the others, the dynamic often disappears.
- **Elevate the protected bowl** so the lurker cannot casually reach it from the floor.
- **Check that your setup closes on absence, not on a timer.** A cover that stays open for a fixed period after the cat leaves is a cover with a scheduled hole in it.

Do not try to solve lurking by punishing the lurker. Interrupting a cat near a food bowl builds anxiety around food, and you will pay for it later in a different form.

## Step 5: Does Each Cat Need Their Own Feeder?

Usually yes, if their food differs — and the reason is not the hardware but the food itself.

A single protected bowl can be registered to several cats. That is useful when two of your three eat the same thing and you only need to exclude the third. It does not work when the two authorised cats are on different diets, because the bowl holds one food at a time.

The practical rule: **one protected bowl per distinct diet**, not per cat.

## Step 6: The First Two Weeks

Expect a settling-in period and plan for it.

**Days 1–3.** Introduce one feeder at a time, not all of them at once. Three new objects appearing simultaneously is a lot for a cautious cat.

**Days 4–10.** Watch full meals, not snapshots. You are looking for who eats where, who waits, and who has quietly stopped eating. If one cat is avoiding their feeder, see [My Cat Is Afraid of the Feeder Lid](/blog/cat-afraid-of-feeder-lid).

**Day 14 onwards.** Weigh everyone. Then weigh them monthly. In a three-cat house the weights are your instrumentation — they will tell you about a cross-feeding problem weeks before anything else does.

## Where This Approach Struggles

Three cats in a small apartment is genuinely hard. If you cannot put meaningful distance between bowls or break sight lines, you are asking the hardware to do a job that layout should be doing. Vertical space is usually the answer in small homes, but not always a sufficient one.

Active food aggression also does not respond to this. If a cat is physically driving another away rather than opportunistically stealing, the feeder protects the food while the underlying problem continues. That needs addressing directly.

And a cat who has learned to slip into an open bowl behind their housemate is doing something no identification system can prevent. Placement, not hardware, is the fix.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How far apart should cat bowls be in a multi-cat home?**
Further than feels necessary, and with something solid between them. Across a room is a reasonable minimum; separate rooms is better. Broken sight lines matter more than raw distance.

**Can three cats share one selective feeder?**
They can share one *bowl* if they eat the same food — most feeders let you register multiple IDs. They cannot share a bowl if their diets differ, because it holds one food at a time.

**My third cat just waits for the others to finish. Is that a problem?**
Only if they are getting food that way. Watch a few full meals. If the lurker is taking from an open bowl after the authorised cat steps back, move that bowl out of sight of the common area and feed the lurker first.

**Should I feed all three cats at the same time?**
For scheduled meals, staggering by a few minutes often reduces tension more than any equipment change — particularly if you feed the most food-motivated cat first.

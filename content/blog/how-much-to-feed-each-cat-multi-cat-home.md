It is 7 a.m. You have one bag of kibble, one plastic scoop and three bowls lined up on the counter. Each bowl gets one level scoop, the way it has for years, maybe a little extra for the big one because he always looks hungry. The small tortie is getting bony along the spine. The big tabby is getting harder to lift. They have all been eating "the same amount."

"How much should I feed my cat?" sounds like a question with one answer printed on the bag. In a multi-cat home it is really three or four questions, one per cat, and the bag can only answer them roughly. This post covers how vets estimate a daily calorie target, how to turn that into grams of your actual food, and why the portion you measure is not always the portion each cat eats.

None of this replaces your vet. If a cat has a medical condition, is losing or gaining weight without explanation, or is on a prescription diet, ask them for that cat's target first.

---

## Why "How Much Should I Feed My Cat" Has a Different Answer for Each Cat

Cats in the same house can need quite different amounts of food. Size is the obvious difference, but neutering and life stage change the number too. The [Merck Veterinary Manual](https://www.merckvetmanual.com/management-and-nutrition/nutrition-small-animals/nutritional-requirements-of-small-animals) gives different multipliers for an intact adult cat, a neutered adult, a cat prone to obesity and a kitten. Four cats can mean four targets.

Getting it wrong is common. The Association for Pet Obesity Prevention's 2022 survey found that [61% of cats were classified as overweight or having obesity](https://www.petobesityprevention.org/2022), and that 28% of owners of those cats described their cat's body condition as "normal." One scoop for every bowl is an easy way to end up there. It may suit one cat, overfeed another and underfeed a third.

## Step 1: Estimate Each Cat's Daily Calories

Vets usually start with the resting energy requirement (RER), the energy a cat uses at rest. Merck gives the formula:

**RER = 70 × (body weight in kg)^0.75**

RER is then multiplied by a life-stage factor. From the Merck table for cats:

- **Intact adult:** 1.4 × RER
- **Neutered adult:** 1.2 × RER
- **Obesity-prone adult:** 1.0 × RER
- **Kitten:** 2.5 × RER (Merck notes kittens can also be fed free choice)

Worked from that formula, typical weights come out like this:

| Cat's weight | RER (kcal/day) | Neutered adult, 1.2 × RER | Obesity-prone, 1.0 × RER |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 kg | about 160 | about 190 | about 160 |
| 4 kg | about 198 | about 238 | about 198 |
| 5 kg | about 234 | about 281 | about 234 |
| 6 kg | about 268 | about 322 | about 268 |

These are estimates, not prescriptions. Merck says any formula for energy needs "should be considered a starting point." The real test is what each cat's weight and shape do over the next few weeks.

### A note on overweight cats

For a cat that is already overweight, don't just shrink the scoop. [VCA Animal Hospitals](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/obesity-in-cats) warns that "it is not appropriate to simply reduce the volume of your cat's current food," because cutting back an ordinary diet can leave the cat short of other nutrients. VCA recommends a calorie-restricted therapeutic diet, with the target set by your vet. Our guide to [helping an overweight cat lose weight when you have multiple cats](/blog/overweight-cat-diet-multiple-cats) covers the household side.

## Step 2: Turn Calories Into Grams of Your Food

A calorie target means nothing until you know what is in your food. US pet food labels carry this under a "Calorie Content" heading. According to [AAFCO](https://www.aafco.org/consumers/understanding-pet-food/reading-labels/), it is stated in kilocalories per kilogram as fed, and often per can, cup or treat as well.

The conversion is one division:

**grams per day = daily kcal ÷ (kcal per kg ÷ 1,000)**

A worked example (the food figures are made up to show the arithmetic, so use the numbers on your own bag or can):

- Your neutered 4 kg cat's target is about 238 kcal a day.
- Your dry food says 3,800 kcal/kg, which is 3.8 kcal per gram.
- 238 ÷ 3.8 ≈ **63 g of kibble a day**, split across however many meals you feed.

If you mix wet and dry, budget in calories. If a wet pouch in this example provides 70 kcal, one pouch leaves 168 kcal for kibble, which is about 44 g of the same food.

### Why the chart on the bag is only a rough guide

The feeding chart on the bag gives a range for a range of weights, written for a typical cat. AAFCO says "feeding directions are guidelines that may need modification to meet a particular pet's needs." Use the chart to sanity-check your number, not to replace it.

## Step 3: Weigh It, Don't Scoop It

This is the step most households skip, and it matters most for cats because their portions are small.

A study by German and colleagues in the *Journal of Animal Physiology and Animal Nutrition* looked at people portioning dry kibble with measuring cups. Accuracy ranged [from an 18% under-estimate to an 80% over-estimate](https://academy.royalcanin.com/en/veterinary/imprecision-when-using-measuring-cups-to-weigh-out-extruded-dry-kibbled-food), and errors got worse as portions got smaller. The authors warned that "over time, such errors could contribute to insidious weight gain."

A digital kitchen scale fixes most of this. Two habits help:

- **Weigh the whole day once.** Each morning, weigh each cat's daily ration into its own labelled container, then split it into meals.
- **Write the numbers down.** "Tortie 55 g, Tabby 50 g, Grey 63 g" on the fridge saves guessing, especially for a sitter. Our [cat sitter feeding instructions](/blog/cat-sitter-feeding-instructions-multiple-cats) post covers what else to write down.

## Step 4: Check the Result Every Few Weeks

The formula gets you close, and the cat tells you the rest. Weigh each cat regularly (VCA suggests monthly weigh-ins for cats on a weight plan) and check body condition by feel, not just the scale. Our post on [checking each cat's body condition](/blog/cat-body-condition-score-multi-cat-home) walks through it.

- **Losing weight it shouldn't?** Raise the portion a little, recheck in a few weeks, and talk to your vet if the loss continues.
- **Gaining weight it shouldn't?** Before cutting the portion, read the next section. The problem may not be the portion.
- **Steady and in good shape?** That is the right amount for this cat, whatever the formula said.

Count treats too. VCA says they should make up [no more than 10% of daily calories](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/obesity-in-cats). For the 238 kcal cat, that is about 24 kcal. If treats go to whoever comes running, see [how to give treats to multiple cats](/blog/how-to-give-treats-to-multiple-cats).

## The Multi-Cat Problem: The Portion You Weigh Isn't the Portion Each Cat Eats

You can get the maths exactly right and still get the wrong result. A carefully weighed 63 g in the grey cat's bowl does nothing if the tabby eats half of it.

Think of it like a household budget where everyone shares one bank card. You can set each allowance to the cent, but if anyone can spend from the shared account, the plan only exists on paper. In a multi-cat home, the shared account is every bowl the faster or bolder cat can reach.

Picture a two-cat flat where the bigger cat finishes breakfast in two minutes, then walks over and takes the rest of the slower eater's bowl. On paper both cats get 240 kcal. In practice one gets far more and the other far less. Seeing the big cat gain weight, the owner cuts his portion, which makes him hungrier and keener to raid the other bowl. If that sounds familiar, read [why your cat is stealing the other cat's food](/blog/cat-stealing-other-cats-food).

So before you change a portion because the scale moved, watch two or three full meals from start to finish. Know who ate what before you adjust the numbers.

### Ways to make each portion reach the right cat

Our comparison of [cat portion control methods](/blog/cat-portion-control-multiple-cats) goes deeper, but in short:

- **Separate rooms with closed doors.** Free and reliable, if you are home to reopen the doors and every cat eats in one sitting.
- **Supervised meals, bowls lifted after a set time.** Good for prompt eaters. It struggles with grazers who leave half and come back later.
- **Height or gap tricks.** A high shelf or a cat-sized gap can help when the size difference is large. Agile cats often get past.
- **Selective feeders.** A feeder that recognises a collar tag or microchip lets a grazer return to its own bowl. It costs more, and some cats need time to accept it.

## When This Won't Work on Its Own

Weighing portions will not solve everything. If a cat is losing weight on its full portion, eating more without gaining, or suddenly drinking or vomiting more, see your vet rather than adjusting the numbers. Kittens, pregnant or nursing cats and cats with kidney disease, diabetes or hyperthyroidism need targets from a vet. And if two cats cannot eat apart without stress or fighting, sort out where and how they eat before you worry about the grams.

## FAQ

### How much should I feed my cat a day?

Vets often start with RER = 70 × (body weight in kg)^0.75, then multiply by 1.2 for a typical neutered adult, according to the Merck Veterinary Manual. For a neutered 4 kg cat that comes to about 238 kcal a day. Divide by your food's calories per gram, then adjust based on the cat's weight over the next few weeks.

### Should all my cats get the same amount of food?

Usually not. Weight, neutering and life stage all change the target, so work out a number for each cat and weigh each portion separately.

### Is it better to measure cat food by cup or by weight?

By weight. One study found measuring-cup portions of kibble ranged from an 18% under-estimate to an 80% over-estimate, with bigger errors for smaller portions.

### How many calories can come from treats?

VCA Animal Hospitals recommends no more than 10% of a cat's daily calories. Subtract treat calories before you weigh out the main food.
